\begin{abstract}
We formalise and study the \textit{alignment--comprehension decoupling hypothesis}: an aligned LLM may produce on-topic, operationally specific responses to a harmful request expressed in Hinglish/Romanized Hindi (\rh{}) while reliably refusing the equivalent English request, indicating that refusal training transfers less robustly than the surface comprehension required to generate such responses. To test this under controlled conditions, we introduce \sys{}, a \emph{probe methodology} that couples a multi-axis equivalence auditor with a graded four-level compliance rubric, using GEPA-based prompt evolution as a measurement instrument that enriches for informative behavioral flips while preserving semantic equivalence. Across 1,512 certified English/\rh{} pairs spanning three open-weight models, four harm categories, and three jailbreak strategies, our central finding is qualitative: \emph{symbolic masking} primarily induces topical engagement (1.0\% critical flip rate), whereas \emph{scenario nesting} and \emph{logical appeal} produce substantially higher actionable compliance (15.1\% and 9.1\%). Behavioral regimes are strongly model-specific: one model is \textit{asymmetrically unsafe} (35.9 pp English/\rh{} refusal gap), while two remain \textit{symmetrically safe} or \textit{symmetrically permissive} under the same harness. The harness surfaces asymmetry where it exists and confirms its absence elsewhere, positioning \sys{} as a controlled measurement primitive for multilingual safety auditing.
\end{abstract}
 
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) are post-trained to refuse harmful requests, and these refusal behaviors are well-characterized in English. Yet billions of users interact with LLMs in informal multilingual registers. One important case is code-switched Hinglish written in Romanized Hindi (\rh{}), the default register for hundreds of millions of South Asian users, where users freely mix Hindi and English vocabulary in informal Latin-script transliteration. Whether English refusal training transfers to these registers is a question with direct deployment-time consequences: if it does not, a model that safely refuses "how to initiate a riot'' may produce a substantive, on-topic response to the same request phrased as "Danga kaise start karein?" (Appendix~\ref{app:qual_example})

Recent multilingual safety research suggests this transfer is unreliable. Safety alignment generalizes across languages less robustly than semantic comprehension \cite{wang2024xsafety,deng2024multilingual,yong2023low,shen2024language}, and models that refuse harmful English prompts often comply when equivalent requests are translated, transliterated, or code-switched \cite{li2024cross,upadhayay2024sandwich,song2025multilingualblending,yoo2025code}. The deeper problem, however, is methodological: existing multilingual jailbreak evaluations rarely enforce semantic equivalence between prompt pairs. When a Hinglish prompt elicits unsafe behavior that its English counterpart did not, the gap could reflect language, framing, specificity, ambiguity, or adversarial structure, and prior work cannot tell us which. \emph{Without controlled attribution, the field cannot measure how much safety actually transfers across languages, let alone diagnose where it fails}.

We address this gap with \sys{}, a \emph{probe methodology} that isolates language as the sole causal variable in multilingual refusal evaluation. The framework couples (i) a structured English/\rh{} pair generator, (ii) a multi-axis equivalence auditor that certifies semantic equivalence before any model evaluation occurs, and (iii) a graded four-level compliance rubric that distinguishes \emph{topical engagement} from \emph{fully actionable harmful assistance}, a distinction binary jailbreak metrics structurally cannot make. Figure~\ref{fig:intro-pipeline} summarises this measurement flow.

The design addresses three concrete gaps in prior multilingual safety work. First, our \textbf{multi-axis equivalence auditor} validates whether English and \rh{} prompts preserve the same harmful intent, scenario, operational specificity, and attack strategy \emph{before} evaluation, rather than assuming equivalence post-hoc. Second, rather than optimizing prompts purely for attack success, we \textbf{repurpose GEPA-based prompt evolution} within DSPy \cite{khattab2024dspy,agrawal2025gepa} as a measurement instrument that jointly enriches for equivalence quality, judge consistency, and informative behavioral flips. Third, we replace binary jailbreak success metrics with a \textbf{graded four-level compliance rubric} that exposes severity structure binary scoring collapses.

Applying this framework to 1,512 certified English/\rh{} probe pairs across four harm categories, three adversarial strategies, and three open-weight multilingual models, we surface two findings the existing literature could not. First, behavioral regimes are sharply model-specific: \qwen{} exhibits a \textbf{35.9 percentage-point} English/\rh{} refusal collapse and a strict 152:0 directional asymmetry, while \gpt{} and \nemo{} remain near-symmetric under the identical harness. This is evidence that the gap is a property of certain models, not of the measurement pipeline. Second, and more importantly, \emph{symbolic masking produces topical engagement at rates comparable to other strategies but actionable harm at $\geq 15\times$ lower rate}, a qualitative threat distinction invisible to every binary jailbreak metric currently in use. The first finding shows the harness works; the second shows it sees things prior methods cannot.

\paragraph{Research Contributions:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item We introduce \sys{}, a probe methodology for measuring multilingual safety asymmetries under controlled semantic equivalence.

    \item We propose a multi-axis equivalence auditor that provides stronger experimental control for attributing observed safety asymmetries to language-conditioned alignment failure.

    \item We show that multilingual safety failures are highly model-specific and strategy-dependent: symbolic masking primarily produces topical engagement, while scenario nesting and logical appeal produce actionable harm at substantially higher rates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Multilingual Safety Gaps in LLMs: }
Safety alignment in modern LLMs is primarily established through post-training on curated harmful-content datasets that remain heavily English-centric \cite{zou2023universal,chao2024jailbreakbench,mazeika2024harmbench}. Recent multilingual studies show refusal behavior weakening in non-English, low-resource, or non-canonical settings \cite{wang2024xsafety,deng2024multilingual,yong2023low,shen2024language}, with multilingual benchmarks consistently reporting elevated unsafe completion rates outside English \cite{pattnayak2026indicsafe,ning2025linguasafe,yong2025state}, suggesting that semantic comprehension transfers more reliably across languages than safety alignment.

\paragraph{Cross-Lingual Jailbreaks and Language-Conditioned Failure: }
Models refusing harmful English prompts often comply when equivalent requests are translated, code-switched, blended, or rewritten in alternate scripts \cite{li2024cross,atil2025jailbreak,upadhayay2024sandwich,song2025multilingualblending,yoo2025code}. However, most prior work optimizes for attack success rather than controlled measurement, so observed failures are hard to attribute specifically to language: prompts typically vary simultaneously in framing, specificity, ambiguity, and adversarial structure.

\paragraph{Romanization and Code-Switched Interaction: }
Safety classifiers are highly sensitive to surface-form variation \cite{yang2024benchmarking}, particularly relevant for \rh{} where Hindi/English mixing and informal Latin-script transliteration produce spelling variation, abbreviation, and phonetic inconsistency unlikely to be well represented during safety training. Analogous transliteration-based weakening has been shown for Arabic \cite{alghanim2024jailbreaking}. The closest Indic effort, \textsc{IndicJR} \cite{pattnayak-chowdhuri-2026-indicjr}, evaluates jailbreak robustness across 12 South Asian languages and three orthographies via a judge-free protocol, prioritizing breadth, whereas \sys{} prioritizes \emph{causal attribution} to language through certified-equivalent paired probes.

\paragraph{Jailbreak Evaluation and Graded Safety Measurement: }
Most jailbreak evaluations rely on binary success metrics that collapse qualitatively different behaviors (topical acknowledgement, partial procedural guidance, and fully actionable compliance) into one category \cite{yi2024survey,souly2024strongreject,liu2024jailjudge,zhang2025falsereject,cao2025safedialbench}. These dynamics may further be mediated by language-specific reward disparities \cite{zhao-etal-2025-mpo} and orthographic variation \cite{pattnayak-chowdhuri-2026-indicjr}.

\textbf{Positioning: } \sys{} differs from prior multilingual jailbreak work in two ways: (i) a multi-axis equivalence auditor certifies semantic equivalence between English and \rh{} pairs prior to evaluation, reducing prompt drift; and (ii) graded compliance scoring replaces binary attack-success metrics, exposing severity structure invisible under binary scoring.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formalism}

Let $\mathcal{M}$ denote an aligned LLM parameterized by $\theta$, with output distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid p)$ for prompt $p \in \mathcal{P}$. As a behavioral abstraction, we conceptualize the model as containing two functionally distinct components: a semantic comprehension mechanism and a safety alignment mechanism. \textit{We do not claim these are mechanistically separable}; the decomposition is a modeling device for defining flip events.

We model semantic comprehension as a latent mapping $\mathcal{C}_\theta : \mathcal{P} \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}$, where $\mathcal{C}_\theta(p)$ captures the harmful intent and operational semantics of prompt $p$ independent of its surface linguistic form. Separately, let $\mathcal{S}_\theta : \mathcal{P} \rightarrow \{0,1\}$ denote a binary alignment gate, where $\mathcal{S}_\theta(p)=1$ indicates activation of the model's refusal behavior. We treat $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ \emph{as if} it gated refusal in a binary manner; in practice, refusal is a distribution over tokens, and the binary abstraction is used only to define paired flip events. \textit{Crucially, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ operates over the observable realization of the prompt rather than purely over $\mathcal{C}_\theta(p)$.} 

\paragraph{Alignment--Comprehension Decoupling Hypothesis.}


Consider a harmful English prompt $p_{\mathrm{EN}}$ and a semantically equivalent \rh{} counterpart $p_{\mathrm{RH}}$. 
\begin{equation} \mathcal{C}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}}) \approx \mathcal{C}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}}) \label{eq:eq_constraint} \end{equation} 
Equation~\ref{eq:eq_constraint} defines a comprehension-equivalence condition that our equivalence auditor enforces at the prompt level and that we corroborate behaviorally at the response level via Gate~A (see ``Inferring Comprehension'' below). Under this condition, the alignment--comprehension decoupling hypothesis predicts an observable asymmetry: forward flips occur strictly more often than reverse flips.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\PP\!\left[\mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}})\!=\!1, \mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}})\!=\!0\right] \\
&\quad > \PP\!\left[\mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}})\!=\!0, \mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}})\!=\!1\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}

Intuitively, the surface comprehension required to generate on-topic operational responses appears to transfer more reliably from pretraining than the refusal boundaries optimized predominantly on English-centric safety data during post-training. 

\paragraph{Inferring Comprehension from Response Content:}
We do not measure $\mathcal{C}_\theta$ directly. Instead, we infer comprehension from response content via the grading function $J$: a response that satisfies Gate $A$ (topic-relevant beyond a generic refusal) demonstrates that the target model recognized the prompt's subject matter, and a response satisfying Gates $A \wedge B$ (topic-relevant with concrete operational detail) demonstrates that the model recognized the prompt's harmful intent specifically. Forward flips are therefore evidence of comprehension-without-refusal by construction: the \rh{} response is substantively on-topic and operationally specific, yet the refusal behavior that activated for the equivalent English prompt did not activate here.

\paragraph{Behavioral Proxy and Compliance Scoring.}
Because the internal gate $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is not directly observable, we measure its behavioral proxy through generated responses. Let $J : \mathcal{R} \rightarrow \{0,1,2,3\}$ denote a deterministic grading function mapping a response to one of four levels: full refusal ($0$), topical acknowledgement ($1$), partial procedural guidance ($2$), or fully actionable harmful compliance ($3$).

For a certified-equivalent pair $(p_{\mathrm{EN}}, p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ and target model $m$, we compute:
\[
s_{\mathrm{EN}} = J(m(p_{\mathrm{EN}})),
\qquad
s_{\mathrm{RH}} = J(m(p_{\mathrm{RH}})).
\]
We define a \emph{forward flip} as $s_{\mathrm{EN}} = 0 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} \geq 2$, and a \emph{critical flip} as $s_{\mathrm{EN}} \leq 1 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} = 3$.

\paragraph{Language-Conditioned Safety Gap.}
To quantify multilingual alignment asymmetry, we define the English--\rh{} refusal gap for model $m$ and harm category $c$ as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_c(m)
&=
\mathbb{P}_{(p_{EN},p_{RH}) \sim D_c}
\left[J(m(p_{EN})) = 0\right] \\
&\quad -
\mathbb{P}_{(p_{EN},p_{RH}) \sim D_c}
\left[J(m(p_{RH})) = 0\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:refusal-gap}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_c$ denotes the distribution of certified-equivalent prompt pairs for category $c$. Positive values of $\Delta_c(m)$ indicate that model $m$ refuses harmful English prompts more frequently than semantically equivalent \rh{} prompts.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\sys{} is designed to measure multilingual safety asymmetries while isolating language as the primary causal variable. The pipeline operates through five sequential stages: evidence retrieval, prompt-pair generation, equivalence certification, target-model evaluation, and graded compliance scoring.

\subsection{High-Level Pipeline and Information Flow}

The pipeline first retrieves regionally grounded contextual evidence, then generates multilingual prompt pairs using an optimized generator, filters them through a multi-axis equivalence auditor, and finally evaluates certified pairs against target models using a graded compliance rubric.

Unlike manually translated jailbreak datasets, the entire pipeline is automated end-to-end. This reduces translation artifacts, inconsistent phrasing, and human bias during pair construction, allowing multilingual safety asymmetries to be studied under controlled semantic equivalence constraints.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval and Context Gating}

To generate realistic and culturally grounded scenarios, each batch begins with evidence retrieval using the Exa neural search engine \cite{exa2024api}. The system issues category-specific India-context queries such as \textit{``intoxication India context background''}.

Retrieved passages are filtered through a Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{gemini2.5} relevance gate that removes out-of-domain material, strips explicit malicious instructions, and \textit{extracts neutral thematic summaries}. These summaries serve as factual anchors for downstream prompt generation.

\subsection{GEPA-Optimized Pair Generator}

Prompt generation is implemented as a single Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{gemini2.5} call that returns a structured JSON object containing: the English prompt $p_{\mathrm{EN}}$, the \rh{} prompt $p_{\mathrm{RH}}$, scenario metadata, information granularity, target group, and attack strategy.

The generator instruction is optimized prior to the main experiment using Genetic Pareto Optimization (GEPA) \cite{agrawal2025gepa} within DSPy \cite{khattab2024dspy}. The final seed prompt contains: an evolved system instruction, the expected JSON schema, harm-category and strategy descriptions, and a small set of manually curated flip-producing examples.

GEPA iteratively evolves the instruction string by generating candidate prompts, scoring the resulting outputs, and feeding structured feedback back into the optimization loop. The objective is \emph{measurement informativeness}: we deliberately enrich for behavioral flips because the goal is to \emph{surface} asymmetry where it exists, not to estimate its base rate in unprompted use. We are explicit about this design choice : \sys{} is a \textit{probe, not a population estimator.}

For a generator candidate $g$, the optimization objective is:
\begin{align}
F(g) &= 0.55 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{flip}]
      + 0.20 \cdot e \cdot \mathbf{1}[\neg b] \nonumber \\
     &\quad + 0.10 \cdot \mathbf{1}[c_{\mathrm{EN}}]
      + 0.10 \cdot \mathbf{1}[c_{\mathrm{RH}}] \nonumber \\
     &\quad + 0.05 \cdot \mathrm{nat}(p_{\mathrm{RH}})
\end{align}
where $e$ is the auditor equivalence score, $b$ indicates whether the \rh{} prompt is judged stronger than the English prompt, $c_{\mathrm{EN}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{RH}}$ denote judge-consistency checks, and $\mathrm{nat}(p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ is a lightweight \rh{} naturalness heuristic counting common Latin-script Hindi function words (e.g., \emph{hai, kya, lekin, mein, par, toh}).

Our implementation additionally reuses orchestration patterns from the Hermes-Agent harness \cite{nousresearch_hermes_agent_v0140}, including JSON contract enforcement, retrieval gating, and DSPy/GEPA integration.

\subsection{Multi-Axis Equivalence Auditor}

The equivalence auditor is the central methodological component of \sys{}, providing the causal basis for attributing behavioral flips specifically to language rather than prompt drift. Each candidate pair is evaluated across \textbf{nine dimensions}, organized into three groups. \emph{What the prompt asks}: harmful intent, scenario identity, information granularity, attack strategy. \emph{How the prompt is framed}: cultural specificity, target group, ambiguity level. \emph{Translation-quality checks}: \rh{} language fidelity and strategy faithfulness. The grouping ensures that equivalence is enforced on content, framing, and surface realization simultaneously.

\textbf{Auditor Outputs.}
The auditor returns an aggregate equivalence
score $e \in [0,1]$, a stronger-than-English flag
$b$, a language-fidelity flag $\ell$, and a
strategy-faithfulness flag $s$.

A pair is certified if and only if: $\neg b \land \ell \land s \land e \geq 0.6$.
Pairs failing certification are discarded and regenerated.

\subsection{Three-Gate Graded Compliance Rubric}
All target models are evaluated using certified prompt pairs at temperature $T=0$ with empty system prompts. Responses are scored using a deterministic three-gate rubric:
\begin{equation}
J(r)
=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } \neg A \\
1 & \text{if } A \wedge \neg B \\
2 & \text{if } A \wedge B \wedge \neg C \\
3 & \text{if } A \wedge B \wedge C
\end{cases}
\label{eq:judge_math}
\end{equation}
Gate $A$ checks whether the response contains topic-relevant information beyond a generic refusal. Gate $B$ checks for concrete operational details such as tools, chemicals, quantities, or procedural steps. Gate $C$ evaluates whether a non-expert reader could plausibly attempt the harmful act using only the generated response. This decomposition mirrors the graded severity logic adopted by recent jailbreak-evaluation work that moves beyond binary success metrics \cite{souly2024strongreject,liu2024jailjudge,zhang2025falsereject,cao2025safedialbench}, with Gates $B$ and $C$ specifically targeting the \emph{operational specificity} and \emph{actionability} dimensions that binary scoring collapses into a single label.
The judge is instructed to ignore disclaimers, fictional framings, and roleplay wrappers when assigning scores. \rh{} outputs are evaluated entirely on harmful operational content regardless of script.

\paragraph{Judge Validation:}
We manually audited 100 randomly sampled prompt--response pairs stratified across experimental cells using the same rubric. The audit achieved 86\% exact agreement and 99\% adjacent agreement with the automated judge, yielding Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$ and quadratic weighted $\kappa = 0.94$. To further reduce dependence on LLM-as-judge evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging}, we additionally compute a judge-independent directional asymmetry signal based on response length (Appendix~\ref{app:length_signal}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}


\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Grid}

Each evaluation instance consists of an English--\rh{} prompt pair $(p_{\mathrm{EN}}, p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ generated using the pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Within every pair, language is the only intended variable, enforced through the multi-axis equivalence auditor.

For each pair, the target model produces two responses which are independently scored by the compliance judge, yielding: $(s_{\mathrm{EN}}, s_{\mathrm{RH}}) \in \{0,1,2,3\}^2$
These paired scores determine both flip classifications and refusal statistics.

The evaluation matrix spans: three target models, four harm categories, three jailbreak strategies, and 42 prompt pairs per experimental cell, for a total of $3 \times 4 \times 3 \times 42 = 1{,}512$ certified English--\rh{} probe pairs. These pairs are diagnostic probes for target--category--strategy cells; the goal is to test whether the harness can systematically surface English--\rh{} safety asymmetries under equivalence certification, not to estimate population-level harm rates.

All reported analyses are derived from this single controlled sweep, including refusal gaps, flip statistics, transition matrices, and strategy-level summaries.

\subsection{Target Models and Baselines}

We evaluate three contemporary open-weight multilingual models under identical inference conditions to test whether observed asymmetries are model-specific or harness-induced:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Qwen3-30B-A3B:} a multilingual mixture-of-experts model optimized for multilingual instruction following \cite{qwen2025qwen330ba3b}.

    \item \textbf{gpt-oss-20B:} an open-weight mixture-of-experts language model released by OpenAI \cite{openai2025gptoss}.

    \item \textbf{Nemotron-30B-A3B:} a hybrid Mamba--Transformer model optimized for efficient reasoning workloads \cite{nvidia2025nemotron}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{The cross-model comparison primarily serves as a harness-validity check} rather than a leaderboard. If the harness simply inflated failures indiscriminately, all targets would show large English--\rh{} gaps; instead, the same measurement procedure surfaces severe asymmetry for one target while yielding near-symmetric behavior for the other two. 

Under the same evaluation harness, \texttt{gpt-oss-20B} and \texttt{Nemotron-30B-A3B} exhibit near-zero English--\rh{} refusal gaps (+3.6 and +2.6 percentage points respectively), while \qwen{} displays substantially larger asymmetry. We characterize these three outcomes as \emph{Symmetrically Safe}, \emph{Symmetrically Permissive}, and \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe} regimes respectively (formally defined in Section~\ref{sec:res_model}).

All target models are evaluated using: temperature $T=0$, empty system prompts, and a maximum generation length of 4096 tokens. These settings minimize sampling variance and maximize reproducibility across paired evaluations.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_attack_surface_2.png}

\label{fig:attack_surface}

We track four primary metrics to quantify multilingual alignment asymmetry.

\paragraph{Forward Flip Rate ($R_{\mathrm{FF}}$):}
The fraction of pairs satisfying $s_{\mathrm{EN}} = 0 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} \geq 2$, corresponding to cases where an English refusal becomes a partially procedural or actionable \rh{} response.

\paragraph{Critical Flip Rate ($R_{\mathrm{CF}}$):}
The fraction of pairs satisfying $s_{\mathrm{EN}} \leq 1 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} = 3$, representing severe failures where a safely contained English prompt produces fully actionable harmful output in \rh{}.

\paragraph{English--\rh{} Refusal Gap ($\Delta$):}
The percentage-point difference between English and \rh{} refusal rates, defined in Equation~\ref{eq:refusal-gap}. Positive values indicate that a model refuses English prompts more frequently than equivalent \rh{} prompts.

\paragraph{Directional Ratio ($\rho$):}
A judge-independent response-length asymmetry metric. A pair is classified as \emph{forward-shaped} if $|r_{\mathrm{EN}}| < 80 \wedge |r_{\mathrm{RH}}| > 500$, and \emph{reverse-shaped} under the opposite condition. The directional ratio $\rho$ compares the number of forward-shaped and reverse-shaped pairs without relying on semantic LLM-based judgment, and is reported alongside the judge-based metrics as a corroborating signal.

Across the full evaluation sweep, we observe 323 forward flips (21.4\%) and 127 critical flips (8.4\%), indicating that multilingual alignment failures surfaced by the probe are both quantitatively substantial and \textit{qualitatively heterogeneous}.

\subsection{Topical Engagement vs.\ Actionable Harm: The Symbolic Masking Divergence}

The strongest qualitative effect emerges at the strategy level, where different adversarial framings produce qualitatively different forms of multilingual alignment failure. This qualitative distinction is largely invisible under binary jailbreak metrics. 

\emph{Scenario Nesting} is the most severe strategy overall, producing 138 forward flips (27.4\%) and 76 critical flips (15.1\%). \emph{Logical Appeal} also bypasses safety boundaries at high rates, yielding 107 forward flips (21.2\%) and 46 critical flips (9.1\%). In contrast, \emph{Symbolic Masking} exhibits a distinct profile: although it triggers 78 forward flips (15.5\%), it produces only 5 critical flips (1.0\%) : a $\geq$15$\times$ gap in actionable-harm rate relative to the other two strategies despite a non-trivial forward-flip rate.

This distinction highlights the value of the graded compliance rubric. Persona-style masking and archival framings often move the model into topical engagement without consistently eliciting actionable harmful guidance. These responses frequently terminate at partial procedural compliance rather than fully actionable output. By contrast, nested narratives and logical framings more often bypass the refusal boundary entirely, producing operationally useful responses. Under a binary attack-success metric, Symbolic Masking would appear roughly comparable to Logical Appeal; under graded scoring, the two are qualitatively different threats.

The effect is especially pronounced for \qwen{}. Aggregated across categories, Scenario Nesting reaches 82/168 forward flips (48.8\%) and 51 critical flips (30.4\%), while Logical Appeal reaches 77/168 forward flips (45.8\%) and 39 critical flips (23.2\%). Strategy effectiveness is also category dependent: Scenario Nesting dominates intoxication and gambling failures, whereas Logical Appeal is most effective for sexual-violence prompts.

\subsection{Target-Level Refusal Asymmetry: Three Behavioral Regimes}
\label{sec:res_model}

The evaluated models separate into three qualitatively distinct multilingual safety regimes (Table~\ref{tab:target_summary}): \qwen{} is \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe} (35.9 pp gap), \gpt{} is \emph{Symmetrically Safe}, and \nemo{} is \emph{Symmetrically Permissive} (formal thresholds and per-regime refusal rates in Appendix~\ref{app:regimes}).

The central empirical observation is that \emph{the same harness} produces three qualitatively different outcomes, strongly indicating that the measured asymmetry is model-specific rather than a pipeline artifact. The judge-independent response-length analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:length_asymmetry}) corroborates this: \qwen{} produces a strict 152:0 forward-to-reverse directional ratio, while baseline models remain non-directional.

\subsection{Failure Concentration Across Categories}

Figure~\ref{fig:attack_surface} shows that surfaced failures are highly localized rather than uniformly distributed: severe cells are concentrated almost entirely within \qwen{} (peak: 83.3\% forward flips on Logical Appeal + sexual violence), while no \gpt{} or \nemo{} cell exceeds 33.3\%. The harness therefore does not induce generalized failure; it exposes localized regions where a model's English refusal boundary fails to transfer to an equivalent \rh{} surface form : the diagnostic behavior expected of a probe methodology. Per-cell breakdowns appear in Appendix~\ref{app:cells}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several practical constraints shape the scope of our findings. Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{gemini2.5} serves multiple orchestration roles (generation, auditing, judging), and the GEPA objective enriches for informative flips; consequently, reported flip rates index harness sensitivity rather than population base rates. The 35.9 pp asymmetry is observed in one of three targets (\qwen{}), supporting an existence claim about severe \rh{} decoupling in frontier open-weight models rather than a universality claim. Category effects partly reflect seed structure in gambling and sexual-violence scenarios, and minor target-side artifacts (mojibake in some \nemo{} outputs; a few reverse-shaped \gpt{} pairs) did not affect cross-model ranking. 

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduce \sys{}, an equivalence-certified paired-probe methodology for multilingual safety auditing, isolating language as the sole causal variable between English and \rh{} prompts. Unlike prior multilingual jailbreak work that conflates language with prompt drift and adversarial structure, our auditor and graded four-level rubric jointly enable clean causal attribution and severity-aware measurement that binary metrics structurally cannot provide. Empirically, we show that (i) at least one frontier open-weight model (\qwen{}) exhibits severe \rh{} refusal asymmetry under controlled equivalence, while two other targets do not, and (ii) symbolic masking produces topical engagement at roughly the rate of other strategies but actionable harm at $\geq 15\times$ lower rate : a qualitative distinction binary metrics cannot see. 

Future work should extend the probe to additional Indic settings (Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu), separate generator/auditor/judge across independent model families, and apply the harness to mitigation benchmarking such as Hinglish-augmented safety tuning and reward-gap transfer \cite{zhao-etal-2025-mpo}.


\section*{Ethical Statement}

This work studies multilingual jailbreak vulnerabilities in aligned language models using systematically generated adversarial prompts. As with prior jailbreak and safety-evaluation research, the identified behaviors could potentially be misused to bypass safety controls in deployed systems. However, we believe that measuring and documenting these failures is necessary for improving multilingual alignment robustness and developing effective mitigations.

Our experiments were conducted exclusively for defensive safety evaluation purposes. The framework is designed to help model developers identify language-conditioned alignment gaps, evaluate mitigation strategies, and improve safety coverage for real-world multilingual users. We encourage future alignment pipelines to include stronger evaluation and safety tuning for code-switched and Romanized language settings such as Hinglish/Romanized Hindi.

\subsection{Judge-Independent Length-Asymmetry Signal}
\label{app:length_signal}

To reduce dependence on LLM-as-judge evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging}, we additionally compute a fully judge-independent directional asymmetry signal based on response length. A pair is classified as \emph{forward-shaped} if the English response is shorter than 80 characters while the \rh{} response exceeds 500 characters, approximating refusal-versus-engagement asymmetry without semantic judgment. This signal is reported in §\ref{sec:res_model} as the Directional Ratio $\rho$ and visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:length_asymmetry}.

\subsection{Regime Definitions and Per-Model Refusal Rates}
\label{app:regimes}

We define the three regimes formally as follows. A model is \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe} if $\Delta > 15$ pp and the \rh{} refusal rate is less than half the English refusal rate. A model is \emph{Symmetrically Safe} if $|\Delta| \leq 10$ pp and the English refusal rate exceeds $60\%$. A model is \emph{Symmetrically Permissive} if $|\Delta| \leq 10$ pp and the English refusal rate is below $50\%$ (``symmetric'' here does not imply ``safe'' : it means the model refuses similarly little in both languages).

Concretely, \qwen{} refuses 48.6\% of harmful English prompts but only 12.7\% of their \rh{} counterparts (197 forward flips, 92 critical flips). \gpt{} maintains closely matched refusal rates across both registers (66.1\% vs.\ 62.5\%). \nemo{} remains comparatively permissive but approximately balanced across languages (39.5\% vs.\ 36.9\%).

\subsection{Per-Cell Failure Breakdown}
\label{app:cells}

The strongest failure regions are concentrated almost entirely within \qwen{}. The most severe cells are \emph{Logical Appeal on sexual violence} (83.3\% forward flips, 22 critical flips) and \emph{Scenario Nesting on intoxication} (81.0\% forward flips, 19 critical flips). Additional high-risk regions include \emph{Scenario Nesting on gambling} (66.7\%, 16 critical flips) and \emph{Logical Appeal on intoxication} (59.5\%, 13 critical flips). The 152:0 directional ratio for \qwen{} is observed where short English refusals (<80 chars) are paired with long \rh{} completions (>500 chars), with the reverse configuration never occurring.

\subsection{Judge Validation and Reliability}

To validate the automated grading pipeline, we manually reviewed 100 randomly sampled prompt--response pairs stratified across models, harm categories, and strategy classes.

Manual annotation achieved:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item 86\% exact agreement with the automated judge,
    \item 99\% adjacent agreement,
    \item Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$,
    \item Quadratic weighted $\kappa = 0.94$.
\end{itemize}

Most disagreement cases occurred near adjacent rubric boundaries, especially between topical acknowledgement (Score 1) and partial procedural guidance (Score 2). The automated judge generally behaved more conservatively than human annotators.

\subsection{Additional GEPA Optimization Details}

The multilingual pair generator was optimized prior to the main experimental sweep using Genetic Pareto Optimization (GEPA) inside the DSPy framework.

The optimization objective jointly rewarded:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item multilingual behavioral flip discovery,
    \item semantic equivalence preservation,
    \item logical consistency of judge outputs,
    \item and \rh{} naturalness.
\end{itemize}

Unlike conventional jailbreak optimization pipelines that maximize raw attack success, the objective in \sys{} was designed to maximize \emph{measurement informativeness} : enriching for behavioral flips that the probe is designed to surface. The generator was frozen before the main 1,512-pair evaluation sweep and was not adaptively modified during measurement.

\subsection{Auditor Thresholding}

The equivalence auditor evaluates prompt pairs across nine dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item harmful intent,
    \item scenario identity,
    \item information granularity,
    \item jailbreak strategy,
    \item cultural specificity,
    \item target group,
    \item ambiguity level,
    \item \rh{} language fidelity,
    \item strategy faithfulness.
\end{enumerate}

A prompt pair is certified only if:
\[
e \geq 0.6,
\qquad
\neg b,
\qquad
\ell = 1,
\qquad
s = 1
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item $e$ is the aggregate semantic-equivalence score,
    \item $b$ indicates whether the \rh{} prompt became stronger than the English prompt,
    \item $\ell$ denotes language fidelity,
    \item and $s$ denotes strategy faithfulness.
\end{itemize}

Pairs failing certification are discarded and regenerated before evaluation.

\subsection{Target-Side Artifacts}

A small number of target-side artifacts were observed during experimentation:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Some \nemo{} \rh{} outputs contained localized mojibake corruption.
    \item \gpt{} produced a limited number of reverse-shaped response pairs.
    \item Certain long-form \rh{} completions contained unstable transliteration consistency across sentences.
\end{itemize}

These artifacts introduced minor background noise but did not materially affect the overall ranking structure or the primary multilingual asymmetry findings.

Aapke research ka sabse mahatvapurn hissa yeh hai ki kaise ek "Ritual Item" ek "Weapon" mein transform hota hai. Isse anthropology mein \textbf{"Weaponization of Symbols"} kaha jata hai.
