\begin{abstract}
We formalize and study the \textit{alignment--comprehension decoupling hypothesis}. An aligned LLM may produce an on-topic, operationally specific response to a harmful request written in Hinglish or Romanized Hindi (\rh{}), while it reliably refuses the equivalent English request. This pattern suggests that refusal training transfers less robustly than the surface comprehension needed to generate such responses. To test this hypothesis under controlled conditions, we introduce \sys{}. This \emph{probe methodology} couples a multi-axis equivalence auditor with a graded four-level compliance rubric. It uses GEPA-based prompt evolution as a measurement instrument that enriches for informative behavioral flips while preserving semantic equivalence. Across 1,512 certified English and \rh{} pairs spanning three open-weight models, four harm categories, and three jailbreak strategies, our central finding is qualitative. \emph{Symbolic masking} primarily induces topical engagement (1.0\% critical flip rate), whereas \emph{scenario nesting} and \emph{logical appeal} produce substantially higher actionable compliance (15.1\% and 9.1\%). Behavioral regimes are strongly model-specific. One model is \textit{asymmetrically unsafe}, with a 35.9 pp English and \rh{} refusal gap, while two remain \textit{symmetrically safe} or \textit{symmetrically permissive} under the same harness. The harness surfaces asymmetry where it exists and confirms its absence elsewhere. An ablation of the certification gate, a cross-family judge agreement check, and a re-audit on three further target models support these claims. We present \sys{} as a controlled measurement methodology for multilingual safety auditing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) are post-trained to refuse harmful requests, and these refusal behaviors are well-characterized in English. Yet billions of users interact with LLMs in informal multilingual registers. One important case is code-switched Hinglish written in Romanized Hindi (\rh{}). This is the default register for hundreds of millions of South Asian users, who freely mix Hindi and English vocabulary in informal Latin-script transliteration. Whether English refusal training transfers to these registers is a question with direct deployment-time consequences. If it does not, a model that safely refuses ``how to initiate a riot'' may produce a substantive, on-topic response to the same request phrased as ``Danga kaise start karein?'' (Appendix~\ref{app:qual_example}).

Recent multilingual safety research suggests this transfer is unreliable. Safety alignment generalizes across languages less robustly than semantic comprehension \cite{wang2024xsafety,deng2024multilingual,yong2023low,shen2024language}, and models that refuse harmful English prompts often comply when equivalent requests are translated, transliterated, or code-switched \cite{li2024cross,upadhayay2024sandwich,song2025multilingualblending,yoo2025code}. The deeper problem, however, is methodological. Existing multilingual jailbreak evaluations rarely enforce semantic equivalence between prompt pairs. When a Hinglish prompt elicits unsafe behavior that its English counterpart did not, the gap could reflect language, framing, specificity, ambiguity, or adversarial structure, and prior work cannot tell us which. \emph{Without controlled attribution, the field cannot measure how much safety actually transfers across languages, let alone diagnose where it fails}.

We address this gap with \sys{}, a \emph{probe methodology} that isolates language as the sole causal variable in multilingual refusal evaluation. The framework couples (i) a structured English and \rh{} pair generator, (ii) a multi-axis equivalence auditor that certifies semantic equivalence before any model evaluation occurs, and (iii) a graded four-level compliance rubric that distinguishes \emph{topical engagement} from \emph{fully actionable harmful assistance}, a distinction binary jailbreak metrics structurally cannot make. Figure~\ref{fig:intro-pipeline} summarizes this measurement flow.

Throughout, we treat \rh{} as the first instantiation of a language-general measurement primitive. The pipeline is language-agnostic apart from two components, a language-fidelity check and a naturalness heuristic, both of which are specific to the target register. Extending the pipeline to other Indic scripts and registers, such as Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, and Urdu, is a stated direction for future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) rather than a claim of this paper.

We give a compact glossary for two terms used throughout. A \emph{forward flip} is a certified pair on which the model refuses the English prompt but produces partial procedural guidance or fully actionable harmful content for the equivalent \rh{} prompt. A \emph{reverse flip} is the mirror-image case. Section~\ref{sec:formalism} gives formal definitions of both terms.

The design addresses three concrete gaps in prior multilingual safety work. First, our \textbf{multi-axis equivalence auditor} validates whether English and \rh{} prompts preserve the same harmful intent, scenario, operational specificity, and attack strategy \emph{before} evaluation, rather than assuming equivalence post-hoc. Second, rather than optimizing prompts purely for attack success, we \textbf{repurpose GEPA-based prompt evolution} within DSPy \cite{khattab2024dspy,agrawal2026gepa} as a measurement instrument that jointly enriches for equivalence quality, judge consistency, and informative behavioral flips. Third, we replace binary jailbreak success metrics with a \textbf{graded four-level compliance rubric} that exposes severity structure binary scoring collapses.

Applying this framework to 1,512 certified English and \rh{} probe pairs across four harm categories, three adversarial strategies, and three open-weight multilingual models, we surface two findings the existing literature could not. First, behavioral regimes are sharply model-specific. \qwen{} exhibits a \textbf{35.9 percentage-point} English and \rh{} refusal collapse and a strict 152:0 directional asymmetry, while \gpt{} and \nemo{} remain near-symmetric under the identical harness. This is evidence that the gap is a property of certain models, not of the measurement pipeline. Second, and more importantly, \emph{symbolic masking produces topical engagement at rates comparable to other strategies, but produces actionable harm at a rate roughly 9 to 15 times lower}. Every binary jailbreak metric currently in use is blind to this qualitative threat distinction. The first finding shows the harness works. The second shows it sees things prior methods cannot.

\paragraph{Research Contributions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item We introduce \sys{}, a probe methodology for measuring multilingual safety asymmetries under controlled semantic equivalence.

    \item We propose a multi-axis equivalence auditor that provides stronger experimental control for attributing observed safety asymmetries to language-conditioned alignment failure.

    \item We show that multilingual safety failures are highly model-specific and strategy-dependent. Symbolic masking primarily produces topical engagement, while scenario nesting and logical appeal produce actionable harm at substantially higher rates.

    \item We validate the pipeline in three ways. An ablation shows that pairs the auditor rejects flip at a substantially higher rate than certified pairs do. An independent judge family reproduces our binary flip metric at 92.86\% agreement. Three additional target models reproduce the model-specific pattern (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multilingual Safety Gaps and Cross-Lingual Jailbreaks}
\label{subsec:rw_multilingual}

Safety alignment in modern LLMs is established mainly through post-training on curated harmful-content datasets that remain heavily English-centric \cite{zou2023universal,chao2024jailbreakbench,mazeika2024harmbench}. A large body of work now shows that this alignment weakens outside English. Studies across many language families report that refusal behavior degrades in non-English, low-resource, and non-canonical settings \cite{wang2024xsafety,deng2024multilingual,yong2023low,shen2024language}, and multilingual benchmarks consistently record elevated unsafe completion rates outside English \cite{pattnayak2026indicsafe,ning2025linguasafe,yong2025state}. A closely related line of work turns this observation into attacks. Models that refuse harmful English prompts often comply when the same request is translated, code-switched, blended across languages, or rewritten in another script \cite{li2024cross,atil2025jailbreak,upadhayay2024sandwich,song2025multilingualblending,yoo2025code}. Taken together, this body of work has established that semantic comprehension transfers across languages more reliably than safety alignment does, and it has established that the gap is large enough to exploit.

What this work has not established is why the gap appears. Prior evaluations compare an English prompt against a non-English prompt without checking that the two ask for the same thing. The two prompts typically differ at once in framing, operational specificity, ambiguity, and adversarial structure, so an observed gap cannot be assigned to language rather than to any of these. We add one constraint that this cluster does not impose. We certify semantic equivalence across nine axes before a pair reaches any target model, which makes language the only intended difference within a pair. This is what licenses us to read a flip as evidence about language rather than about prompt drift, and Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} tests that certification step empirically rather than assuming it.

\subsection{Romanization, Code-Switching, and Indic Safety Resources}
\label{subsec:rw_indic}

Safety classifiers are sensitive to surface-form variation \cite{yang2024benchmarking}, which matters for \rh{}, where Hindi and English mixing and informal Latin-script transliteration produce spelling variation, abbreviation, and phonetic inconsistency unlikely to be well represented in safety training. Transliteration-based weakening has been demonstrated for Arabic and Arabizi \cite{ghanim2024jailbreaking}. Two recent resources cover South Asian languages directly. \textsc{IndicSafe} \cite{pattnayak2026indicsafe} evaluates safety across 12 Indic languages using culturally grounded prompts that native speakers translated, and reports substantial cross-language drift. \textsc{IndicJR} \cite{pattnayak2026indicjr} evaluates jailbreak robustness across 12 South Asian languages and three orthographies under a judge-free protocol, and finds that romanization \emph{reduces} contract-bound jailbreak success. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} places both alongside the general benchmarks.

Both resources prioritize breadth across languages, and neither certifies that the compared prompts are equivalent. \textsc{IndicSafe} relies on human translation quality and \textsc{IndicJR} on a transliteration engine, so in both cases equivalence is assumed by construction rather than verified per pair. We trade breadth for control by covering one register and certifying every pair. Our directional result also appears to run against \textsc{IndicJR}, and three differences reconcile them. \textsc{IndicJR} compares native script against romanized script within a single language, whereas we compare English against \rh{}. Its romanization penalty appears in a contract-bound track, and its authors attribute the effect to tokenization and schema validity rather than to alignment. In its unconstrained track, which is the setting closest to ours, refusals largely collapse, so we read the two results as complementary.

\subsection{Jailbreak Evaluation, Graded Scoring, and Judge Reliability}
\label{subsec:rw_eval}

Most jailbreak evaluations optimize for attack success and report it with a binary metric. Automated attack search is the dominant paradigm. Tree of Attacks with Pruning \cite{mehrotra2024tap} is representative. An attacker model iteratively refines candidate prompts and prunes unpromising branches until the target is jailbroken, which achieves high success rates with few queries. Standardized benchmarks then score the resulting attacks \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench,mazeika2024harmbench,yi2024survey}. The scoring step collapses topical acknowledgement, partial procedural guidance, and fully actionable compliance into one label, and a growing line of work argues that this loses information that matters \cite{souly2024strongreject,liu2024jailjudge,zhang2025falsereject,cao2025safedialbench}. Because graded scoring at scale needs an automatic judge, evaluation increasingly depends on LLM-as-judge protocols \cite{zheng2023judging}, whose reliability is itself now under scrutiny. \citet{mukherjee2026geometry} show across Indic datasets and 41 judges that LLM judges agree with one another more than they agree with humans, and that their evaluation axis is close to orthogonal to the human one.

Our use of prompt evolution differs from this cluster in purpose rather than in mechanism. Attack-optimization methods such as \citet{mehrotra2024tap} search for prompts that succeed, whereas we use the same class of search to find prompts that are \emph{informative}, subject to a hard equivalence gate that the search cannot relax. We also adopt graded rather than binary scoring, which is what surfaces the symbolic-masking result that a binary metric would hide. On judge reliability we treat \citet{mukherjee2026geometry} as a caution against our own evidence rather than as support for it, so we report human agreement separately in Appendix~\ref{app:judge_validation} and make a narrower claim from the cross-judge check in Section~\ref{subsec:interrater}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formalism}

Let $\mathcal{M}$ denote an aligned LLM parameterized by $\theta$, with output distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid p)$ for prompt $p \in \mathcal{P}$. As a behavioral abstraction, we conceptualize the model as containing two functionally distinct components, a semantic comprehension mechanism and a safety alignment mechanism. \textit{We do not claim that these two components are mechanistically separable}. The decomposition is a modeling device we use to define flip events.

We model semantic comprehension as a latent mapping $\mathcal{C}_\theta : \mathcal{P} \rightarrow \mathcal{Z}$, where $\mathcal{C}_\theta(p)$ captures the harmful intent and operational semantics of prompt $p$ independent of its surface linguistic form. Separately, let $\mathcal{S}_\theta : \mathcal{P} \rightarrow \{0,1\}$ denote a binary alignment gate, where $\mathcal{S}_\theta(p)=1$ indicates activation of the model's refusal behavior. We treat $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ \emph{as if} it gated refusal in a binary manner. In practice, refusal is a distribution over tokens. We use the binary abstraction only to define paired flip events. \textit{Crucially, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ operates over the observable realization of the prompt rather than purely over $\mathcal{C}_\theta(p)$.}

\paragraph{Alignment--Comprehension Decoupling Hypothesis.}

Consider a harmful English prompt $p_{\mathrm{EN}}$ and a semantically equivalent \rh{} counterpart $p_{\mathrm{RH}}$.
\begin{equation} \mathcal{C}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}}) \approx \mathcal{C}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}}) \label{eq:eq_constraint} \end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:eq_constraint} defines a comprehension-equivalence condition that our equivalence auditor enforces at the prompt level. We corroborate it behaviorally at the response level via Gate~A, described under ``Inferring Comprehension'' below. Under this condition, the alignment--comprehension decoupling hypothesis predicts an observable asymmetry. Forward flips occur strictly more often than reverse flips.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\PP\!\left[\mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}})\!=\!1, \mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}})\!=\!0\right] \\
&\quad > \PP\!\left[\mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{EN}})\!=\!0, \mathcal{S}_\theta(p_{\mathrm{RH}})\!=\!1\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}

Intuitively, the surface comprehension required to generate on-topic operational responses appears to transfer more reliably from pretraining than the refusal boundaries do. Those boundaries are optimized predominantly on English-centric safety data during post-training.

\paragraph{Inferring Comprehension from Response Content.}
We do not measure $\mathcal{C}_\theta$ directly. Instead, we infer comprehension from response content via the grading function $J$. A response that satisfies Gate $A$, meaning it is topic-relevant beyond a generic refusal, demonstrates that the target model recognized the prompt's subject matter. A response satisfying Gates $A \wedge B$, meaning it is topic-relevant with concrete operational detail, demonstrates that the model recognized the prompt's harmful intent specifically. Forward flips are therefore evidence of comprehension without refusal by construction. The \rh{} response is substantively on-topic and operationally specific, yet the refusal behavior that activated for the equivalent English prompt did not activate here.

\paragraph{Behavioral Proxy and Compliance Scoring.}
Because the internal gate $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is not directly observable, we measure its behavioral proxy through generated responses. Let $J : \mathcal{R} \rightarrow \{0,1,2,3\}$ denote a deterministic grading function mapping a response to one of four levels: full refusal ($0$), topical acknowledgement ($1$), partial procedural guidance ($2$), or fully actionable harmful compliance ($3$).

For a certified-equivalent pair $(p_{\mathrm{EN}}, p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ and target model $m$, we compute:
\[
s_{\mathrm{EN}} = J(m(p_{\mathrm{EN}})),
\qquad
s_{\mathrm{RH}} = J(m(p_{\mathrm{RH}})).
\]
We define a \emph{forward flip} as $s_{\mathrm{EN}} = 0 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} \geq 2$, and a \emph{critical flip} as $s_{\mathrm{EN}} \leq 1 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} = 3$. We define a \emph{reverse flip} as $s_{\mathrm{EN}} \geq 2 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} = 0$. This is the mirror-image case, in which the model produces a partially or fully actionable English response but refuses the equivalent \rh{} prompt outright.

\paragraph{Language-Conditioned Safety Gap.}
To quantify multilingual alignment asymmetry, we define the English and \rh{} refusal gap for model $m$ and harm category $c$ as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_c(m)
&=
\mathbb{P}_{(p_{EN},p_{RH}) \sim D_c}
\left[J(m(p_{EN})) = 0\right] \\
&\quad -
\mathbb{P}_{(p_{EN},p_{RH}) \sim D_c}
\left[J(m(p_{RH})) = 0\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:refusal-gap}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_c$ denotes the distribution of certified-equivalent prompt pairs for category $c$. Positive values of $\Delta_c(m)$ indicate that model $m$ refuses harmful English prompts more frequently than semantically equivalent \rh{} prompts.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We first give the intuition that the rest of this section makes precise. Appendix~\ref{app:qual_example} shows a concrete certified pair. The model refuses the English member outright, while the equivalent \rh{} member receives a long and operationally detailed response. The question we formalize is whether directional failures of this kind are attributable to the language shift itself, rather than to incidental differences between the two prompts.

Our core testable claim is directional asymmetry. Given certified-equivalent pairs, comprehension transfers across the English-to-\rh{} shift more reliably than the refusal boundary does. Forward flips should therefore exceed reverse flips. The formalism below is the machinery we use to state and test this claim. The contribution it licenses is a causal reading of the measured gap rather than a purely descriptive one.

\sys{} measures multilingual safety asymmetries while isolating language as the primary causal variable. The pipeline operates through five sequential stages: evidence retrieval, prompt-pair generation, equivalence certification, target-model evaluation, and graded compliance scoring.

\subsection{High-Level Pipeline and Information Flow}

The pipeline first retrieves regionally grounded contextual evidence. It then generates multilingual prompt pairs using an optimized generator, filters them through a multi-axis equivalence auditor, and finally evaluates certified pairs against target models using a graded compliance rubric.

Unlike manually translated jailbreak datasets, the entire pipeline is automated end-to-end. This reduces translation artifacts, inconsistent phrasing, and human bias during pair construction. It also lets us study multilingual safety asymmetries under controlled semantic equivalence constraints.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval and Context Gating}
\label{subsec:retrieval}

To generate realistic and culturally grounded scenarios, each batch begins with evidence retrieval using the Exa neural search engine \cite{exa2024api}. The system issues category-specific India-context queries such as \textit{``intoxication India context background''}.

We filter retrieved passages through a Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{geminiteam2025gemini} relevance gate that removes out-of-domain material, strips explicit malicious instructions, and \textit{extracts neutral thematic summaries}. These summaries serve as factual anchors for downstream prompt generation.

\subsection{GEPA-Optimized Pair Generator}
\label{subsec:generator}

Prompt generation is implemented as a single Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{geminiteam2025gemini} call that returns a structured JSON object containing the English prompt $p_{\mathrm{EN}}$, the \rh{} prompt $p_{\mathrm{RH}}$, scenario metadata, information granularity, target group, and attack strategy.

We optimize the generator instruction before the main experiment using Genetic Pareto Optimization, or GEPA \cite{agrawal2026gepa}, within DSPy \cite{khattab2024dspy}. The final seed prompt contains an evolved system instruction, the expected JSON schema, harm-category and strategy descriptions, and a small set of manually curated flip-producing examples.

GEPA iteratively evolves the instruction string by generating candidate prompts, scoring the resulting outputs, and feeding structured feedback back into the optimization loop. The objective is \emph{measurement informativeness}. We deliberately enrich for behavioral flips because the goal is to \emph{surface} asymmetry where it exists, not to estimate its base rate in unprompted use. We are explicit about this design choice. \sys{} is a \textit{probe, not a population estimator}.

For a generator candidate $g$, the optimization objective is:
\begin{align}
F(g) &= 0.55 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{flip}]
      + 0.20 \cdot e \cdot \mathbf{1}[\neg b] \nonumber \\
     &\quad + 0.10 \cdot \mathbf{1}[c_{\mathrm{EN}}]
      + 0.10 \cdot \mathbf{1}[c_{\mathrm{RH}}] \nonumber \\
     &\quad + 0.05 \cdot \mathrm{nat}(p_{\mathrm{RH}})
\label{eq:gepa_objective}
\end{align}
where $e$ is the auditor equivalence score, $b$ indicates whether the \rh{} prompt is judged stronger than the English prompt, $c_{\mathrm{EN}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{RH}}$ denote judge-consistency checks, and $\mathrm{nat}(p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ is a lightweight \rh{} naturalness heuristic counting common Latin-script Hindi function words (e.g., \emph{hai, kya, lekin, mein, par, toh}).

We state our reasons for these weights explicitly. Equation~\ref{eq:gepa_objective} governs only the generator during the pre-sweep GEPA optimization phase, and we freeze the generator before evaluation. It is not an evaluation metric, and it does not relax the hard certification gate defined in Section~\ref{subsec:auditor}.

The ordering of the weights reflects measurement priorities rather than attack optimization. Flip discovery receives the dominant weight of 0.55 because surfacing behavioral flips is the purpose of the probe. Equivalence quality is next at 0.20 because it is the validity constraint that makes any surfaced flip attributable to language. Judge consistency on each side at 0.10, and \rh{} naturalness at 0.05, act as regularizers. They keep proposals well-formed and gradeable without dominating the search.

Every pair still passes the same hard certification gate regardless of these weights. The soft weighting therefore steers the search toward regions of the prompt space where informative flips are more likely to appear, and it does not change which pairs count as valid. Appendix~\ref{app:gepa_details} describes the optimization procedure in more detail.

Our implementation additionally reuses orchestration patterns from the Hermes-Agent harness \cite{nousresearch2026hermes}, including JSON contract enforcement, retrieval gating, and DSPy and GEPA integration.

\subsection{Multi-Axis Equivalence Auditor}
\label{subsec:auditor}

The equivalence auditor is the central methodological component of \sys{}. It provides the causal basis for attributing behavioral flips specifically to language rather than to prompt drift. The auditor evaluates each candidate pair across \textbf{nine dimensions}, organized into three groups. The first group covers what the prompt asks, and contains harmful intent, scenario identity, information granularity, and attack strategy. The second group covers how the prompt is framed, and contains cultural specificity, target group, and ambiguity level. The third group covers translation quality, and contains \rh{} language fidelity and strategy faithfulness. This grouping lets us enforce equivalence on content, framing, and surface realization at the same time.

\textbf{Auditor Outputs.}
The auditor returns an aggregate equivalence score $e \in [0,1]$, a stronger-than-English flag $b$, a language-fidelity flag $\ell$, and a strategy-faithfulness flag $s$.

A pair is certified if and only if $\neg b \land \ell \land s \land e \geq 0.6$. The pipeline discards and regenerates any pair that fails certification. Appendix~\ref{app:auditor_thresholds} lists the nine dimensions and the exact certification condition. Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} reports an ablation that tests whether this gate does real work.

\subsection{Three-Gate Graded Compliance Rubric}
\label{subsec:rubric}
We evaluate all target models on certified prompt pairs at temperature $T=0$, with empty system prompts. We score responses using a deterministic three-gate rubric:
\begin{equation}
J(r)
=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } \neg A \\
1 & \text{if } A \wedge \neg B \\
2 & \text{if } A \wedge B \wedge \neg C \\
3 & \text{if } A \wedge B \wedge C
\end{cases}
\label{eq:judge_math}
\end{equation}
Gate $A$ checks whether the response contains topic-relevant information beyond a generic refusal. Gate $B$ checks for concrete operational details such as tools, chemicals, quantities, or procedural steps. Gate $C$ evaluates whether a non-expert reader could plausibly attempt the harmful act using only the generated response. This decomposition mirrors the graded severity logic adopted by recent jailbreak-evaluation work that moves beyond binary success metrics \cite{souly2024strongreject,liu2024jailjudge,zhang2025falsereject,cao2025safedialbench}. Gates $B$ and $C$ specifically target the \emph{operational specificity} and \emph{actionability} dimensions that binary scoring collapses into a single label.
The judge is instructed to ignore disclaimers, fictional framings, and roleplay wrappers when assigning scores. We evaluate \rh{} outputs entirely on harmful operational content regardless of script.

\paragraph{Judge Validation.}
We manually audited 100 randomly sampled prompt--response pairs stratified across experimental cells using the same rubric. The audit achieved 86\% exact agreement and 99\% adjacent agreement with the automated judge, yielding Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$ and quadratic weighted $\kappa = 0.94$. Appendix~\ref{app:judge_validation} gives the full agreement breakdown. To further reduce dependence on LLM-as-judge evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging}, we additionally compute a judge-independent directional asymmetry signal based on response length (Appendix~\ref{app:length_signal}). Section~\ref{subsec:interrater} extends this validation to a second judge model family.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Grid}
\label{subsec:grid}

Each evaluation instance consists of an English and \rh{} prompt pair $(p_{\mathrm{EN}}, p_{\mathrm{RH}})$ generated using the pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Within every pair, language is the only intended variable, enforced through the multi-axis equivalence auditor.

For each pair, the target model produces two responses, and the compliance judge scores each one independently, yielding $(s_{\mathrm{EN}}, s_{\mathrm{RH}}) \in \{0,1,2,3\}^2$. These paired scores determine both flip classifications and refusal statistics.

The evaluation matrix spans three target models, four harm categories, three jailbreak strategies, and 42 prompt pairs per experimental cell. This gives a total of $3 \times 4 \times 3 \times 42 = 1{,}512$ certified English and \rh{} probe pairs. These pairs are diagnostic probes for target, category, and strategy cells. The goal is to test whether the harness can systematically surface English and \rh{} safety asymmetries under equivalence certification, not to estimate population-level harm rates.

All reported analyses are derived from this single controlled sweep, including refusal gaps, flip statistics, transition matrices, and strategy-level summaries.

\subsection{Target Models and Baselines}
\label{subsec:targets}

We evaluate three contemporary open-weight multilingual models under identical inference conditions. This tests whether observed asymmetries are model-specific or harness-induced.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Qwen3-30B-A3B:} a multilingual mixture-of-experts model optimized for multilingual instruction following \cite{qwen2025qwen3}.

    \item \textbf{gpt-oss-20B:} an open-weight mixture-of-experts language model released by OpenAI \cite{openai2025gptoss}.

    \item \textbf{Nemotron-30B-A3B:} a hybrid Mamba--Transformer model optimized for efficient reasoning workloads \cite{nvidia2025nemotron}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{The cross-model comparison primarily serves as a harness-validity check} rather than a leaderboard. If the harness simply inflated failures indiscriminately, all targets would show large English and \rh{} gaps. Instead, the same measurement procedure surfaces severe asymmetry for one target, while it yields near-symmetric behavior for the other two.

Under the same evaluation harness, \gpt{} and \nemo{} exhibit near-zero English and \rh{} refusal gaps, at +3.6 and +2.6 percentage points respectively, while \qwen{} displays substantially larger asymmetry. We characterize these three outcomes as \emph{Symmetrically Safe}, \emph{Symmetrically Permissive}, and \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe} regimes respectively, and we define them formally in Section~\ref{sec:res_model}.

We evaluate all target models using temperature $T=0$, empty system prompts, and a maximum generation length of 4096 tokens. These settings minimize sampling variance and maximize reproducibility across paired evaluations. Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints} lists the exact model identifiers.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

We track four primary metrics to quantify multilingual alignment asymmetry.

\paragraph{Forward Flip Rate ($R_{\mathrm{FF}}$).}
The fraction of pairs satisfying $s_{\mathrm{EN}} = 0 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} \geq 2$. These are cases where an English refusal becomes a partially procedural or actionable \rh{} response.

\paragraph{Critical Flip Rate ($R_{\mathrm{CF}}$).}
The fraction of pairs satisfying $s_{\mathrm{EN}} \leq 1 \wedge s_{\mathrm{RH}} = 3$. These are severe failures where a safely contained English prompt produces fully actionable harmful output in \rh{}.

\paragraph{English and \rh{} Refusal Gap ($\Delta$).}
The percentage-point difference between English and \rh{} refusal rates, defined in Equation~\ref{eq:refusal-gap}. Positive values indicate that a model refuses English prompts more frequently than equivalent \rh{} prompts.

\paragraph{Directional Ratio ($\rho$).}
A judge-independent response-length asymmetry metric. A pair is \emph{forward-shaped} if $|r_{\mathrm{EN}}| < 80 \wedge |r_{\mathrm{RH}}| > 500$, and \emph{reverse-shaped} under the opposite condition. The directional ratio $\rho$ compares the number of forward-shaped and reverse-shaped pairs without relying on semantic LLM-based judgment. We report it alongside the judge-based metrics as a corroborating signal.

\subsection{Topical Engagement versus Actionable Harm}
\label{sec:res_strategy}

The strongest qualitative effect emerges at the strategy level, where different adversarial framings produce qualitatively different forms of multilingual alignment failure. This qualitative distinction is largely invisible under binary jailbreak metrics.

\emph{Scenario Nesting} is the most severe strategy overall, producing 138 forward flips (27.4\%) and 76 critical flips (15.1\%). \emph{Logical Appeal} also bypasses safety boundaries at high rates, yielding 107 forward flips (21.2\%) and 46 critical flips (9.1\%). In contrast, \emph{Symbolic Masking} exhibits a distinct profile. Although it triggers 78 forward flips (15.5\%), it produces only 5 critical flips (1.0\%). This is a gap of roughly 9 to 15 times in actionable-harm rate relative to the other two strategies, despite a non-trivial forward-flip rate.

This distinction highlights the value of the graded compliance rubric. Persona-style masking and archival framings often move the model into topical engagement without consistently eliciting actionable harmful guidance. These responses frequently terminate at partial procedural compliance rather than fully actionable output. By contrast, nested narratives and logical framings more often bypass the refusal boundary entirely, producing operationally useful responses. Under a binary attack-success metric, Symbolic Masking would appear roughly comparable to Logical Appeal. Under graded scoring, the two are qualitatively different threats.

The effect is especially pronounced for \qwen{}. Aggregated across categories, Scenario Nesting reaches 82 of 168 forward flips (48.8\%) and 51 critical flips (30.4\%). Logical Appeal reaches 77 of 168 forward flips (45.8\%) and 39 critical flips (23.2\%). Strategy effectiveness is also category dependent. Scenario Nesting dominates intoxication and gambling failures, whereas Logical Appeal is most effective for sexual-violence prompts. Appendix~\ref{app:qual_extra} gives worked examples of both patterns.

\subsection{Target-Level Refusal Asymmetry: Three Behavioral Regimes}
\label{sec:res_model}

The evaluated models separate into three qualitatively distinct multilingual safety regimes (Table~\ref{tab:target_summary}). \qwen{} is \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe}, with a 35.9 pp gap. \gpt{} is \emph{Symmetrically Safe}. \nemo{} is \emph{Symmetrically Permissive}. Appendix~\ref{app:regimes} gives formal thresholds and per-regime refusal rates.

The central empirical observation is that \emph{the same harness} produces three qualitatively different outcomes. This strongly indicates that the measured asymmetry is model-specific rather than a pipeline artifact. The judge-independent response-length analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:length_asymmetry}) corroborates this. \qwen{} produces a strict 152:0 forward-to-reverse directional ratio, while baseline models remain non-directional.

\subsection{Failure Concentration Across Categories}
\label{sec:res_cells}

Figure~\ref{fig:attack_surface} shows that surfaced failures are highly localized rather than uniformly distributed. Severe cells are concentrated almost entirely within \qwen{}, peaking at 83.3\% forward flips on Logical Appeal with sexual violence, while no \gpt{} or \nemo{} cell exceeds 33.3\%. The harness therefore does not induce generalized failure. It exposes localized regions where a model's English refusal boundary fails to transfer to an equivalent \rh{} surface form. This is the diagnostic behavior we expect of a probe methodology. Per-cell breakdowns appear in Appendix~\ref{app:cells}.

\section{Robustness and Generalization Checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

We report three checks on the pipeline's key design choices and on the generality of our headline finding.\footnote{All robustness checks in this section use the same model checkpoints as the main sweep.} The first ablates the equivalence certification gate. The second tests the compliance judge against an independent judge family. The third re-audits three additional target models. Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} gives full per-cell breakdowns for all three.

\subsection{Certification Ablation}
\label{subsec:ablation}

The equivalence certification gate is the component that licenses our causal reading. We therefore test whether it does real work, rather than discarding pairs at random. We re-ran the full twelve-batch sweep, four harm categories across three target models, with the gate disabled. The equivalence auditor still scored every candidate pair and logged whether that pair would have been certified. We then allowed every candidate to proceed to target-model evaluation regardless of that score.

This design lets us compare two groups drawn from the same uncertified run. The first group contains pairs the auditor would have accepted. The second contains pairs it would have rejected. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the comparison. Pairs the auditor would have rejected flip at 47.4\%, compared with \verify{18.9}\% for pairs it would have accepted. Critical flips follow the same pattern, at 23.5\% against \verify{8.0}\%.

The pairs the auditor screens out are therefore substantially more likely to produce large English and \rh{} score divergence. These pairs fail at least one of the auditor's semantic axes, most often harmful intent, language fidelity, or strategy faithfulness. Divergence they produce more plausibly reflects drift between the two prompt versions than a genuine cross-lingual safety failure. This ablation does not prove that every certified flip is caused by language. It does show that removing certification inflates the flip rate in the direction we would expect if certification filters non-equivalent pairs rather than filtering at random.

Free-tier rate limiting on OpenRouter truncated some \nemo{} runs in this ablation, which contain between 40 and 74 pairs per category instead of 120. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} pools across all models and categories. Readers should interpret the per-model breakdowns in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation_runs} with this imbalance in mind.

\subsection{Inter-Rater Reliability}
\label{subsec:interrater}

Our main results rely on Gemini-2.5-Flash as the compliance judge. We therefore test whether the headline flip metric depends on that specific judge. We re-scored 3,024 responses, which is the full set of 1,512 certified pairs scored on both language sides, using an independent judge from a different model family, NVIDIA NIM \texttt{meta/llama-3.1-70b-instruct}. Target models, attack strategies, harm categories, and languages were identical to the main sweep. Only the judge model differs.

Table~\ref{tab:interrater} reports agreement at two levels. The first is the exact four-level score. The second is the binary pass threshold of $J(r) \geq 2$ that underlies our flip metric. At the binary threshold, the two judges agree on 92.86\% of responses, with Cohen's $\kappa = 0.86$. At the exact four-level score, agreement falls to 70.24\% with $\kappa = 0.55$. The lower exact agreement mainly reflects the two judges drawing boundaries between adjacent severity levels differently, rather than disagreeing about which responses are unsafe overall.

Our flip and critical-flip metrics both use the binary threshold defined in Section~\ref{sec:formalism}. Agreement at that threshold is therefore the relevant reliability check for our headline results. The NIM judge assigns the highest severity score more often than Gemini does, which is consistent with its higher overall pass rate of 54.8\% against 47.6\%.

We note that agreement between two LLM judges is weaker evidence than it appears. \citet{mukherjee2026geometry} show that LLM judges agree with each other more than they agree with humans, so cross-judge agreement can reflect shared bias rather than shared accuracy. We therefore rest the reliability claim on the human validation in Appendix~\ref{app:judge_validation}, where agreement reaches 86\% with $\kappa = 0.79$. We read the cross-judge check as showing only that our headline metric does not depend on one specific judge model. Appendix~\ref{app:interrater_dist} reports the score distributions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduce \sys{}, an equivalence-certified paired-probe methodology for multilingual safety auditing that isolates language as the sole causal variable between English and \rh{} prompts. Prior multilingual jailbreak work conflates language with prompt drift and adversarial structure. Our auditor and graded four-level rubric together support stronger causal attribution and severity-aware measurement than binary metrics allow. Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} gives empirical support for this attribution, because pairs the auditor would reject flip at a substantially higher rate than certified pairs do.

Empirically, we show two things. First, at least one frontier open-weight model, \qwen{}, exhibits severe \rh{} refusal asymmetry under controlled equivalence, while two other targets do not. Second, symbolic masking produces topical engagement at roughly the rate of other strategies, but produces actionable harm at a rate roughly 9 to 15 times lower. Binary metrics cannot see this qualitative distinction.

Future work should extend the probe to additional Indic settings such as Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, and Urdu. It should also separate the generator, auditor, and judge across independent model families, and apply the harness to mitigation benchmarking such as Hinglish-augmented safety tuning and reward-gap transfer \cite{zhao2025mpo}.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several practical constraints shape the scope of our findings. Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{geminiteam2025gemini} serves multiple orchestration roles in our pipeline, namely generation, auditing, and judging. The GEPA objective also enriches for informative flips. Reported flip rates therefore index harness sensitivity rather than population base rates. Section~\ref{subsec:interrater} shows that an independent judge family reproduces our binary flip metric, which partly mitigates the single-judge dependency described here. That check also shows that exact severity levels are less stable across judges than the pass threshold is, so individual four-level scores should be read with more caution than the flip metric.

Our human validation covers 100 sampled pairs, and our cross-judge check covers all 3,024 responses. The larger check is the weaker one, because agreement between LLM judges need not indicate agreement with human raters \cite{mukherjee2026geometry}. A larger human-annotated slice would strengthen the reliability claim more than a third automatic judge would.

The 35.9 pp asymmetry is observed in one of three main-sweep targets, \qwen{}. This supports an existence claim about severe \rh{} decoupling in frontier open-weight models rather than a universality claim. The re-audit in Section~\ref{subsec:reaudit} adds three further targets, all of which fall below \qwen{}, and none of those runs completed all 1,512 pairs because of API rate limits.

Category effects partly reflect seed structure in gambling and sexual-violence scenarios. Minor target-side artifacts, including mojibake in some \nemo{} outputs and a few reverse-shaped \gpt{} pairs, did not affect cross-model ranking. Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts} describes these artifacts. Rate limiting also truncated several \nemo{} runs in the certification ablation, as noted in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}.

Finally, this work covers a single language pair. We treat \rh{} as the first instantiation of a language-general primitive, but we have not yet demonstrated that the pipeline transfers to other Indic scripts and registers.

\section*{Ethical Statement}

This work studies multilingual jailbreak vulnerabilities in aligned language models using systematically generated adversarial prompts. As with prior jailbreak and safety-evaluation research, the identified behaviors could potentially be misused to bypass safety controls in deployed systems. However, we believe that measuring and documenting these failures is necessary for improving multilingual alignment robustness and developing effective mitigations.

Our experiments were conducted exclusively for defensive safety evaluation purposes. The framework is designed to help model developers identify language-conditioned alignment gaps, evaluate mitigation strategies, and improve safety coverage for real-world multilingual users. We encourage future alignment pipelines to include stronger evaluation and safety tuning for code-switched and Romanized language settings such as Hinglish and Romanized Hindi.

\subsection{Judge-Independent Length-Asymmetry Signal}
\label{app:length_signal}

To reduce dependence on LLM-as-judge evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging}, we additionally compute a fully judge-independent directional asymmetry signal based on response length. A pair is \emph{forward-shaped} if the English response is shorter than 80 characters while the \rh{} response exceeds 500 characters. This approximates refusal-versus-engagement asymmetry without semantic judgment. We report this signal in Section~\ref{sec:res_model} as the Directional Ratio $\rho$, and we visualize it in Figure~\ref{fig:length_asymmetry}.

\subsection{Regime Definitions and Per-Model Refusal Rates}
\label{app:regimes}

We define the three regimes formally as follows. A model is \emph{Asymmetrically Unsafe} if $\Delta > 15$ pp and the \rh{} refusal rate is less than half the English refusal rate. A model is \emph{Symmetrically Safe} if $|\Delta| \leq 10$ pp and the English refusal rate exceeds $60\%$. A model is \emph{Symmetrically Permissive} if $|\Delta| \leq 10$ pp and the English refusal rate is below $50\%$. Here, ``symmetric'' does not imply ``safe.'' It means only that the model refuses similarly little in both languages.

Concretely, \qwen{} refuses 48.6\% of harmful English prompts but only 12.7\% of their \rh{} counterparts, with 197 forward flips and 92 critical flips. \gpt{} maintains closely matched refusal rates across both registers, at 66.1\% against 62.5\%. \nemo{} remains comparatively permissive but approximately balanced across languages, at 39.5\% against 36.9\%.

\subsection{Per-Cell Failure Breakdown}
\label{app:cells}

The strongest failure regions are concentrated almost entirely within \qwen{}. The most severe cells are \emph{Logical Appeal on sexual violence}, with 83.3\% forward flips and 22 critical flips, and \emph{Scenario Nesting on intoxication}, with 81.0\% forward flips and 19 critical flips. Additional high-risk regions include \emph{Scenario Nesting on gambling}, at 66.7\% with 16 critical flips, and \emph{Logical Appeal on intoxication}, at 59.5\% with 13 critical flips. We observe the 152:0 directional ratio for \qwen{} where short English refusals under 80 characters are paired with long \rh{} completions over 500 characters. The reverse configuration never occurs.

\subsection{Judge Validation and Reliability}
\label{app:judge_validation}

To validate the automated grading pipeline, we manually reviewed 100 randomly sampled prompt--response pairs stratified across models, harm categories, and strategy classes.

Manual annotation achieved:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item 86\% exact agreement with the automated judge,
    \item 99\% adjacent agreement,
    \item Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$,
    \item Quadratic weighted $\kappa = 0.94$.
\end{itemize}

Most disagreement cases occurred near adjacent rubric boundaries, especially between topical acknowledgement (Score 1) and partial procedural guidance (Score 2). The automated judge generally behaved more conservatively than human annotators. This human comparison, rather than the cross-judge comparison in Section~\ref{subsec:interrater}, is the primary reliability evidence for our rubric.

\subsection{Additional GEPA Optimization Details}
\label{app:gepa_details}

We optimized the multilingual pair generator before the main experimental sweep using Genetic Pareto Optimization (GEPA) inside the DSPy framework.

The optimization objective jointly rewarded:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item multilingual behavioral flip discovery,
    \item semantic equivalence preservation,
    \item logical consistency of judge outputs,
    \item and \rh{} naturalness.
\end{itemize}

Unlike conventional jailbreak optimization pipelines that maximize raw attack success, the objective in \sys{} maximizes \emph{measurement informativeness}, which means enriching for the behavioral flips the probe is designed to surface. We froze the generator before the main 1,512-pair evaluation sweep, and we did not modify it adaptively during measurement.

\subsection{Auditor Thresholding}
\label{app:auditor_thresholds}

The equivalence auditor evaluates prompt pairs across nine dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item harmful intent,
    \item scenario identity,
    \item information granularity,
    \item jailbreak strategy,
    \item cultural specificity,
    \item target group,
    \item ambiguity level,
    \item \rh{} language fidelity,
    \item strategy faithfulness.
\end{enumerate}

A prompt pair is certified only if:
\[
e \geq 0.6,
\qquad
\neg b,
\qquad
\ell = 1,
\qquad
s = 1
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item $e$ is the aggregate semantic-equivalence score,
    \item $b$ indicates whether the \rh{} prompt became stronger than the English prompt,
    \item $\ell$ denotes language fidelity,
    \item and $s$ denotes strategy faithfulness.
\end{itemize}

The pipeline discards and regenerates any pair that fails certification, before evaluation begins.

Aap apne thesis mein \textbf{Emile Durkheim} (Collective Effervescence) aur \textbf{Victor Turner} (Liminality) ke theories ka istemal kar sakte hain taaki aap apne observations ko ek academic framework de sakein.''
