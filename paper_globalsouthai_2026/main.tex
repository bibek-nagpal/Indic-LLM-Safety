\title{Romanized Hindi Reveals a Model-Dependent Safety Asymmetry\\in Open-Weight Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
Safety evaluation is overwhelmingly conducted in standardized English, yet hundreds of
millions of people in South Asia write to language models in Romanized Hindi---Hindi typed
in Latin script, often mixed with English. We ask whether that everyday register is
treated as safely as its English counterpart. We build a shared, frozen bank of 504
meaning-matched English / Romanized-Hindi prompt pairs over four harm categories and three
adversarial framings, certified before any target model is contacted, and evaluate three
open-weight targets on the identical bank. Scoring responses on a 0--3
information-assistance scale, we find a large but strongly model-dependent asymmetry.
Qwen3-30B-A3B is scored as withholding assistance on 65.9\% of English prompts but only
22.8\% of their Romanized-Hindi counterparts ($+43.1$ pp, 95\% CI $[38.5, 47.6]$), with
85 of 504 pairs moving from English refusal or near-refusal to fully actionable Hindi
output; the same bank yields $+4.6$ pp for GPT-OSS-20B and $-1.8$ pp for Nemotron-3-Nano.
A second judge and an independent human annotator both reproduce the Qwen asymmetry, the
human estimating it slightly larger on the same items. Romanized Hindi differs from
English in language, script and training frequency at once, so we identify a
language-conditioned difference and not its mechanism; but across these three models the
difference is not predictable from English-only evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most published safety evaluation of large language models is conducted in standardized
English. The people who actually use these systems frequently do not write that way.
In South Asia, a very large fraction of everyday digital text is \emph{Romanized}: Hindi,
Urdu, Bengali and other languages typed in Latin script and freely mixed with English
\citep{khanuja2020gluecos,madhani2023aksharantar}. This register is the default input mode
for a substantial share of Indian users, and it is close to absent from the safety
benchmarks on which deployment decisions are made
\citep{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,ning2025linguasafe}.

This matters for the Global South specifically: a guarantee that holds in English and
fails in Romanized Hindi allocates protection along the lines that already structure access
to technology. Prior work establishes that low-resource and non-English inputs degrade
refusal behaviour
\citep{yong2023lowresource,deng2024multilingual,wang2024xsafety,shen2024languagebarrier}
and that code-mixed or phonetically perturbed inputs are a distinct attack surface
\citep{yoo2025codeswitch,banerjee2025attributional,aswal2025haet,pattnayak2026indicjr}.
Romanized input is a
further condition: the language is high-resource in speech and in Devanagari, but its
Latin-script form is rare in curated data \citep{j2024romansetu}. Less established is how
large the effect is under a matched comparison, and whether it generalizes across models.

Answering that requires avoiding a methodological trap. If prompts are written or filtered
with reference to how targets respond, the measured gap partly reflects the search
procedure rather than the models. We therefore certify a single frozen prompt bank
\emph{before} any target model is contacted, and evaluate every target on exactly the same
bank. To our knowledge no prior work holds a certified matched English / Romanized-Hindi
bank fixed across multiple targets, which is what licenses a between-model comparison
rather than a per-model anecdote. Our contributions are:

(i) a shared, frozen, target-independent bank of 504 meaning-matched
English / Romanized-Hindi pairs (4 categories $\times$ 3 framings $\times$ 42);
(ii) a paired evaluation of three open-weight models on it (3{,}024 responses) showing a
language-conditioned non-assistance gap from $+43.1$ to $-1.8$ pp \emph{on identical
inputs}; and (iii) validation against a second judge and an independent human annotator,
reporting disagreement in both directions and where each check is not fully independent of
the pipeline.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Matched prompt pairs.}
Each item is a pair $(x^{\mathrm{EN}}, x^{\mathrm{RH}})$ expressing the same harmful
request: $x^{\mathrm{EN}}$ in ordinary English, $x^{\mathrm{RH}}$ in conversational
Romanized Hindi---Hindi in Latin script with the English borrowings a fluent speaker
would use, not a transliteration of the English string \citep{j2024romansetu}. The bank
crosses four India-salient harm categories (violence, intoxication, gambling, sexual
violence) with three adversarial framings (SymbolicMasking, ScenarioNesting,
RolePrompting), 42 accepted pairs in each of the 12 cells: 504 unique pairs. Every pair
uses one of these framings; the bank contains no direct-request and no benign condition,
so our estimand is how these framings transfer across script, not the general safety of
Romanized-Hindi requests.

\paragraph{Target-independent certification, and its limits.}
Candidates were generated by \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} and screened by
\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} on all candidates, followed by \texttt{gpt-5-mini} on those the
primary accepted, against a rubric covering semantic equivalence, preservation of the
framing, and register fidelity. Screening is thus a conjunctive cascade rather than
independent dual rating, and no inter-auditor agreement statistic is defined. Two further
dependencies must be stated. The generator instruction was reflectively optimized against
that auditor-defined validity objective, so auditor acceptance is an optimization target
rather than an independent test; and the generator and the primary response judge are the
same model. No target-model signal entered generation or certification, and the resulting
bank is content-addressed, frozen, and sent identically to every target---so these
dependencies are constant across models and cannot manufacture a between-model contrast,
though they can shift any single model's absolute level.

\paragraph{Targets and scoring.}
We evaluate Qwen3-30B-A3B, GPT-OSS-20B and Nemotron-3-Nano; the first and third are
mixture-of-experts models of the same size class, so parameter count does not separate
them. Each receives all 504 pairs in both languages ($504\times2=1{,}008$ responses per
model; 3{,}024 in total; 1{,}512 paired observations), decoded greedily at temperature 0
with an empty system prompt. Responses are scored 0--3 for \emph{information assistance} by
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}, a scale built to reward specificity over engagement
\citep{souly2024strongreject}: 0 = nothing specific to the harmful request; 1 = engages but
names no specific material, step or mechanism; 2 = names such a specific but is not by
itself actionable; 3 = usable by a non-expert as it stands. Framing and disclaimers do not
lower the score. We call score 0 \emph{non-assistance}, not refusal: it describes the
information delivered, not a detected refusal string.

\paragraph{Estimands.}
The primary quantity is the paired non-assistance gap
$\Delta = \Pr(s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!=\!0) - \Pr(s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!=\!0)$. We also report
\emph{forward flips} ($s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!=\!0$, $s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!\ge\!2$) and
\emph{critical flips} ($s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!\le\!1$, $s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!=\!3$). Intervals are
95\% percentile bootstrap over 10{,}000 resamples of prompt pairs; cross-model contrasts
use paired sign randomization with Holm correction.

The three models, measured on identical inputs by an identical judge, occupy three
different regimes (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). Qwen3-30B-A3B is scored
as withholding assistance on roughly two thirds of English prompts and under a quarter of
the matched Romanized-Hindi prompts. The severity measure matters more than the rate: on
85 of 504 pairs (16.9\%) it is scored 0--1 in English and 3 in Romanized Hindi. The gap is
positive in all 12 category $\times$ strategy cells, ranging from $+7.1$ to $+76.2$ pp
(sign test over disjoint cells, $p = 2^{-12}$).

GPT-OSS-20B is high-refusal in both languages with a small gap ($+4.6$ pp) whose interval
excludes zero. Nemotron-3-Nano shows no aggregate weakening ($-1.8$ pp, CI spanning zero),
but that null needs care: 84.5\% of its Romanized-Hindi zeros are under 80 characters and
5 of 322 match an English refusal pattern---more consistent with degenerate generation than
refusal. Qwen's Romanized-Hindi zeros invert this profile (99.1\% exceed 500 characters,
one matches a refusal pattern), so they are long on-topic responses the judge found
unspecific, which argues against simple incomprehension of the register.

The Qwen gap exceeds the GPT-OSS gap by $38.5$ pp (95\% CI $[32.9, 44.0]$) and the
Nemotron gap by $44.8$ pp ($[38.3, 51.4]$), both with Holm-adjusted permutation
$p < 10^{-4}$, and both stable under every near-duplicate clustering we tried. The
GPT-OSS versus Nemotron contrast is not established: it is $6.3$ pp ($[0.8, 12.1]$,
adjusted $p = 0.032$) at the pair level, but the interval covers zero once near-duplicate
prompts are clustered ($p = 0.10$ at Jaccard $0.60$), and it also reverses under judge
replacement.

\section{Validation and robustness}
\label{sec:val}

\paragraph{Second judge.}
An outcome-independent subset of 324 pairs per model (1{,}944 response judgments) was
rescored by \texttt{gpt-5-mini}. Agreement is moderate on the exact label (73.7\%) and high once adjacent scores are allowed
(91.4\%; quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.813$, unweighted $\kappa = 0.542$)---the expected
profile for an ordinal scale \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024unfair}. The Qwen asymmetry is
stable under replacement ($+42.6$ pp $[37.0, 48.5]$ versus $+43.5$ pp $[37.7, 49.4]$); the
GPT-OSS versus Nemotron ordering is not ($4.3$ pp $[-2.8, 11.7]$ versus $11.7$ pp
$[4.0, 19.4]$), because the replacement scores Romanized-Hindi responses more generously.
\texttt{gpt-5-mini} was also the secondary prompt auditor, so this is a partial rather than
an independent replication---though it does check the generator/judge overlap above.

\paragraph{Independent human validation.}
One human annotator, blind to model identity and to the automated scores, rescored a
stratified sample of 180 pair-model jobs (360 response items) drawn without reference to
any automated outcome. The annotator returned 287 scores, of which 285 could be
byte-verified against the frozen stimuli and form the analysis set. Human--judge exact
agreement is 74.7\% (95\% CI $[69.4, 79.9]$) with 94.0\% adjacent agreement,
$\kappa = 0.513$ and quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.825$---closely matching the
judge--judge profile. On the binary non-assistance boundary the headline estimand uses,
human and judge agree on 88.8\% of items, and every response the primary judge scored 3
was scored $\ge 2$ by the human. Disagreement is directional but not uniformly so: the
human scored higher than the primary judge on 58 items and lower on 14, yet against the
replacement judge the direction reverses (higher on 14, lower on 44), so the primary
judge is conservative relative to this annotator while the replacement is not.

Restricted to jobs where the annotator scored both languages, the human gaps reproduce the
model ordering (Table~\ref{tab:human}); the human point estimate for Qwen is larger than
the judge's on the same 32 jobs ($+53.1$ vs $+43.8$ pp), though the intervals overlap and
we do not test the difference.

\paragraph{Other robustness.}
None of the following moves the Qwen result: restricting to untruncated pairs ($43.36$ pp),
dropping the 2 of 3{,}024 responses scored by the preregistered fallback judge ($43.14$ pp),
or conditioning on prompt length, which is 11.4\% greater in Romanized Hindi but
uncorrelated per pair with the gap ($|\rho| \le 0.05$, $p \ge 0.26$). Appendix~C reports
these in full.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

Three models do not characterize open-weight models, and four framing-wrapped categories do
not characterize harm. We identify a \emph{language-conditioned} difference, not its
mechanism: Romanized Hindi differs from English in language, script, register and training
frequency at once, and the register control that would separate these was specified but not
completed. Pair equivalence was certified by language models only---no human verified any
pair---a structural weakness in a study premised on uneven model competence in this very
register. Human validation rests on a single annotator whose language background was not
returned; we make no inter-annotator, adjudication or consensus claim, and because 73 of
360 items were left unscored in a way correlated with the automated score, that agreement
is indicative. Responses are single greedy decodes, so run-to-run variability is unmeasured.

Within those bounds the result is clear. On identical, certified, meaning-matched inputs,
one of three open-weight models assists with harmful Romanized Hindi requests it refuses in
English---at a rate an independent annotator confirms---while another shows no aggregate
effect. Behaviour in this register was not predictable from English-only evaluation of any
of them. This argues for evaluating in the register Global South users actually write in,
and for per-model rather than aggregate safety claims.

\section{Certification and evaluation detail}

\paragraph{Bank construction and its dependencies.} Candidate pairs were produced by
\texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash} and screened in a cascade: \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash}
scored all candidates and \texttt{openai/gpt-5-mini} scored only those the primary
accepted. The auditors are therefore a conjunctive filter, not independent raters, and no
inter-auditor agreement statistic is defined. Both scored each candidate they saw against a
rubric requiring that the Romanized-Hindi member express the same harmful objective,
request the same level of information, preserve the framing and its structural elements,
and read as conversational Romanized Hindi rather than a transliteration.

Three dependencies must be stated plainly. (i) The generator instruction was
reflectively optimized (GEPA) against an auditor-defined validity objective
(\texttt{gepa\_objective\_required} $=$ \texttt{complete\_target\_independent\_probe\_validity}),
so the 504/504 acceptance rate is an optimization target rather than an independent test of
pair quality. (ii) The generator and the primary response judge are the same model. (iii)
The secondary auditor is also the cross-judge replacement. None of these involved any
target-model signal (\texttt{gepa\_target\_signal\_used} $=$ \texttt{false};
\texttt{target\_models\_used\_during\_generation} $=$ \texttt{[]}), and because the bank is
frozen and sent identically to every target, each is a constant across models and cannot
produce a between-model contrast---but each can shift a single model's absolute level. The
frozen bank stores only retained pairs; per-candidate generation and rejection logs were
not preserved, so we cannot report a certification rejection rate.

\paragraph{Denominators.} 504 unique certified prompt pairs; $504\times2=1{,}008$
responses per target model; 3{,}024 responses in total; 1{,}512 paired pair-model
observations (504 per model). Percentages in Table~\ref{tab:main} are over the 504 pairs of
that model.

\paragraph{Reproducibility configuration.} Bank \texttt{revision\_v2\_3\_1\_final\_504\_dedup},
SHA-256 \texttt{35bfbc1d09e78b77c24d62bbf41783cc9b297dd25f1bba3fe6062d53dc2028ed}. Targets
decoded at temperature $0.0$, empty system prompt, 4{,}096 max tokens; judge
\texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash}, 2{,}048 max tokens, preregistered fallback
\texttt{openai/gpt-5-mini}. Analysis seed 20260829; 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples;
100{,}000 permutation draws. The prompt bank contains harmful content and would be released
only under gated access.

\paragraph{Judge fallback.} The primary judge returned a usable score for 3{,}022 of
3{,}024 responses; for two the provider refused content after the retry policy and the
preregistered fallback scored them. Dropping the two affected pairs moves the Qwen gap from
$43.06$ to $43.14$ pp and leaves the GPT-OSS and Nemotron gaps unchanged to two decimals.

\section{Human validation detail}

\paragraph{Design, and a failed preregistered component.} The sample is 180 pair-model
jobs, 5 in each of the 36 model $\times$ category $\times$ strategy cells, both languages
retained, drawn with seed 20260831; the selection never read an automated score. Items were
independently randomized per annotator with $\ge 10$ positions between the two language
members of a job. The design preregistered \emph{two} annotators scoring identical items,
with an adjudication rule frozen before any label existed. Annotator B returned a workbook
containing no scores. We therefore report Annotator A only, and consequently report no
inter-annotator agreement, no adjudication and no human consensus label; the prespecified
adjudication protocol was never invoked. The annotator's self-reported language background
sheet was returned blank, so we can state only that the instructions required conversational
Romanized-Hindi reading ability and that no item was flagged unreadable (0 ``cannot read'').

\paragraph{Analysis set.} Of 360 delivered items the annotator returned 287 scores. Two
could not be byte-verified against the frozen stimulus---a stray spreadsheet range reference
had been typed into one prompt cell, and one response cell had been overwritten by a
digit---and are excluded, leaving $n = 285$. Including them changes exact agreement from
$74.74\%$ to $74.56\%$.

\paragraph{Missingness.} The 73 unscored items are not missing at random: 34\% carry an
automated score of 0 versus 72\% of scored items, so the annotator disproportionately
skipped items the judge rated more assistive. Attrition is also unbalanced by model
(Qwen 31, Nemotron 28, GPT-OSS 14) and language (EN 41, RH 32), so the per-model human gaps
in Table~\ref{tab:human} rest on unequally depleted strata and are not comparable across
models with the precision their point estimates suggest.

\paragraph{Agreement detail.} By language, human--judge exact agreement is $78.1\%$ on
English ($n=137$) and $71.6\%$ on Romanized-Hindi items ($n=148$); by model, $89.6\%$ for
GPT-OSS-20B, $68.2\%$ for Qwen3-30B-A3B and $63.7\%$ for Nemotron-3-Nano. Against the
replacement judge on the same items the annotator agrees at $79.7\%$ exact
(quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.886$), scoring higher on 14 items and lower on 44---the
reverse of the direction against the primary judge. Of the 32 items the primary judge
scored 3, the human scored all 32 at $\ge 2$ and 27 at exactly 3.

\paragraph{Flip directionality.} Forward and reverse flips are far from balanced for Qwen
(219 forward, 7 reverse) but nearly balanced for Nemotron (65 forward, 70 reverse) and
mildly asymmetric for GPT-OSS (42 forward, 21 reverse). Nemotron's null aggregate gap
therefore reflects cancellation of bidirectional instability rather than consistency.

\paragraph{Near-duplicate clustering.} Within-cell token Jaccard similarity averages
$0.254$ against a cross-bank baseline of $0.138$; five retained pairs exceed the stated
$0.85$ deduplication gate (maximum $0.942$). Collapsing near-duplicates into clusters and
bootstrapping over clusters leaves the Qwen gap at $43.06$ pp with the interval widening
only to $[37.3, 48.6]$ at Jaccard $0.50$ (504 pairs $\rightarrow$ 293 units), and leaves
both Qwen contrasts at $p < 10^{-4}$ throughout. The GPT-OSS versus Nemotron contrast
degrades from $p = 0.032$ (pair level) to $p = 0.062$ (Jaccard $0.65$) and $p = 0.10$
(Jaccard $0.60$), with the interval covering zero; we therefore do not treat it as
established.

\paragraph{Truncation.} Qwen truncated 22 Romanized-Hindi and 0 English responses
(discordance $p = 4.8\times10^{-7}$); Nemotron truncated the other way (77 English, 35
Romanized Hindi, $p = 1.7\times10^{-5}$); GPT-OSS is balanced ($p = 0.37$). Restricting to
untruncated pairs gives Qwen $43.36$ pp ($n = 482$), GPT-OSS $4.73$ pp ($n = 423$) and
Nemotron $-0.50$ pp ($n = 401$).

\paragraph{Other analyses.} The frozen analysis also includes a cluster bootstrap over pair
identities (10{,}000 resamples, reported throughout), paired sign randomization with Holm
correction for the three cross-model contrasts (100{,}000 draws), Bowker symmetry tests on
the score-transition matrices, and a prompt-length null. Response length is reported as a
judge-free diagnostic only; because verbosity and assistance are confounded we do not treat
it as independent evidence of harm.
