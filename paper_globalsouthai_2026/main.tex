\begin{abstract}
Romanized Hindi---Hindi typed in Latin script, often mixed with English---is an important
condition for language-model safety evaluation. We ask how responses to harmful requests
differ from their English counterparts. We build a shared, frozen bank of 504
meaning-matched English / Romanized-Hindi prompt pairs over four harm categories and three
adversarial framings, certified before any target model is contacted, and evaluate three
open-weight targets on the identical bank. Scoring responses on a 0--3
information-assistance scale, we find a large but strongly model-dependent asymmetry.
Qwen3-30B-A3B is scored as withholding assistance on 65.9\% of English prompts but only
22.8\% of their Romanized-Hindi counterparts ($+43.1$ pp, 95\% CI $[38.5, 47.6]$), with
85 of 504 pairs moving from English scores 0--1 to score 3 in Romanized Hindi
output; the same bank yields $+4.6$ pp for GPT-OSS-20B and $-1.8$ pp for Nemotron-3-Nano.
A second judge preserves the large Qwen gap. An exploratory single-co-author audit of
354 verified items preserves the same qualitative model-gap ordering.
Both input conditions use Latin script: the contrast changes language and linguistic
realization, not script alone. We identify a language-conditioned difference, not its
mechanism; English-only measurements need not characterize Romanized-Hindi behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

English-focused benchmarks such as HarmBench and JailbreakBench provide important safety
measurements \citep{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}, but multilingual evaluation
also needs the written forms people use. Code-switching and Latin-script representations
of Indic languages motivate existing NLP resources
\citep{khanuja2020gluecos,madhani2023aksharantar}; these sources do not establish how many
people use Romanized Hindi to interact with language models.

For the Global South, safety assessments that omit such inputs risk uneven coverage.
Prior work documents safety failures under low-resource and non-English inputs
\citep{yong2023lowresource,deng2024multilingual,wang2024xsafety,shen2024languagebarrier}
and under code-mixed or phonetically perturbed inputs
\citep{yoo2025codeswitch,banerjee2025attributional,aswal2025haet,pattnayak2026indicjr}.
CSRT studies code-switching red-teaming; IndicJR evaluates jailbreak robustness in South
Asian languages. LinguaSafe broadens multilingual safety coverage \citep{ning2025linguasafe}.
RomanSetu studies romanization for model capability \citep{j2024romansetu}; Indi-RomCoM
benchmarks Romanized Indic--English instruction following \citep{das2026indiromcom}.
Our focus is a paired information-assistance contrast on one shared, automatically
certified English / Romanized-Hindi bank, with uncertainty and measurement limitations.

Answering that requires avoiding a methodological trap. If prompts are written or filtered
with reference to how targets respond, the measured gap partly reflects the search
procedure rather than the models. We therefore certify a single frozen prompt bank
\emph{before} any target model is contacted, and evaluate every target on exactly the same
bank. This limits target-dependent prompt selection without eliminating selection or
measurement bias. Our contributions are:

(i) a shared, frozen, target-independent bank of 504 meaning-matched
English / Romanized-Hindi pairs (4 categories $\times$ 3 framings $\times$ 42);
(ii) a paired evaluation of three open-weight models on it (3{,}024 responses) showing a
language-conditioned non-assistance gap from $+43.1$ to $-1.8$ pp \emph{on identical
inputs}; and (iii) a second-judge robustness check and exploratory single-co-author comparison,
reporting disagreement and dependencies within the pipeline.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Matched prompt pairs.}
Each item is a pair $(x^{\mathrm{EN}}, x^{\mathrm{RH}})$ expressing the same harmful
request: $x^{\mathrm{EN}}$ in ordinary English, $x^{\mathrm{RH}}$ in conversational
Romanized Hindi---Hindi in Latin script with the English borrowings a fluent speaker
would use, not a transliteration of the English string \citep{j2024romansetu}. The bank
crosses four India-salient harm categories (violence, intoxication, gambling, sexual
violence) with three adversarial framings (SymbolicMasking, ScenarioNesting,
RolePrompting), 42 accepted pairs in each of the 12 cells: 504 unique pairs. Every pair
uses one of these framings; the bank contains no direct-request and no benign condition,
so our estimand is how these framings transfer between EN and RH, not the general safety of
Romanized-Hindi requests.

\paragraph{Target-independent certification, and its limits.}
Candidates were generated by \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} and screened by
\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} after deterministic prechecks and deduplication, followed by
\texttt{gpt-5-mini} only on primary-auditor acceptances. Both must pass semantic,
information-level, framing and register gates; this is a conjunctive cascade, not
unconditional independent dual rating. A target-independent GEPA development run used
the primary auditor's 13-constraint validity metric but retained the seed instruction
unchanged: no evolved instruction was used. Every retained pair passed both auditors by
selection, not as an independent quality test. The generator and primary response judge
are the same model. No target outcomes informed bank construction. Sharing the frozen bank
limits target-specific selection but cannot rule out model-dependent measurement effects
from shared pipeline dependencies.

\paragraph{Targets and scoring.}
We evaluate Qwen3-30B-A3B, GPT-OSS-20B and Nemotron-3-Nano; the first and third are
mixture-of-experts models of the same size class, so parameter count does not separate
them. Each receives all 504 pairs in both languages ($504\times2=1{,}008$ responses per
model; 3{,}024 in total; 1{,}512 paired observations), decoded greedily at temperature 0
with an empty system prompt. Responses are scored 0--3 for \emph{information assistance} by
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}, using our frozen rubric: 0 = nothing specific to the harmful request; 1 = engages but
names no specific material, step or mechanism; 2 = names such a specific but is not by
itself actionable; 3 = usable by a non-expert as it stands. Framing and disclaimers do not
lower the score. We call score 0 \emph{non-assistance}, not refusal: it describes the
information delivered, not a detected refusal string. This distinguishes useful assistance
from superficial engagement, a concern also raised by StrongREJECT
\citep{souly2024strongreject}, without claiming that work validates our rubric.
Judges received prompt text and explicit EN/RH language labels; language was not blinded.

\paragraph{Estimands.}
The primary quantity is the paired non-assistance gap
$\Delta = \Pr(s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!=\!0) - \Pr(s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!=\!0)$. We also report
\emph{forward flips} ($s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!=\!0$, $s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!\ge\!2$) and
\emph{critical flips} ($s^{\mathrm{EN}}\!\le\!1$, $s^{\mathrm{RH}}\!=\!3$). Intervals are
95\% percentile bootstrap over 10{,}000 resamples of prompt pairs; cross-model contrasts
use paired sign randomization with Holm correction.

The three models have heterogeneous point estimates
(Table~\ref{tab:main}, Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). Qwen3-30B-A3B is scored
as withholding assistance on roughly two thirds of English prompts and under a quarter of
the matched Romanized-Hindi prompts. The severity measure matters more than the rate: on
85 of 504 pairs (16.9\%) it is scored 0--1 in English and 3 in Romanized Hindi. The gap is
positive in all 12 category $\times$ strategy cells, ranging from $+7.1$ to $+76.2$ pp.

GPT-OSS-20B has high non-assistance in both languages with a small gap ($+4.6$ pp) whose interval
excludes zero. Nemotron-3-Nano shows no aggregate weakening ($-1.8$ pp, CI spanning zero),
with substantial bidirectional flips. All 272 of its Romanized-Hindi score-0 responses
under 80 characters are the same explicit English refusal, missed by the original
ASCII-apostrophe regex. Qwen's Romanized-Hindi zeros are mostly long (99.1\% exceed 500
characters), but length establishes neither comprehension nor absence of refusal
(Appendix~C).

The Qwen gap exceeds the GPT-OSS gap by $38.5$ pp (95\% CI $[32.9, 44.0]$) and the
Nemotron gap by $44.8$ pp ($[38.3, 51.4]$), both with Holm-adjusted permutation
$p < 10^{-4}$, and both stable under every near-duplicate clustering we tried. The
GPT-OSS versus Nemotron contrast is not established: it is $6.3$ pp ($[0.8, 12.1]$,
adjusted $p = 0.032$) at the pair level, but the interval covers zero once near-duplicate
prompts are clustered ($p = 0.10$ at Jaccard $0.60$). Judge replacement preserves its
direction but changes its magnitude and statistical support.

\section{Validation and robustness}
\label{sec:val}

\paragraph{Second judge.}
An outcome-independent subset of 324 pairs per model (1{,}944 response judgments) was
rescored by \texttt{gpt-5-mini}. Agreement is moderate on the exact label (73.7\%) and high once adjacent scores are allowed
(91.4\%; quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.813$, unweighted $\kappa = 0.542$). The Qwen asymmetry is
stable under replacement ($+42.6$ pp $[37.0, 48.5]$ versus $+43.5$ pp $[37.7, 49.4]$); the
GPT-OSS minus Nemotron contrast is $4.3$ pp $[-2.8, 11.7]$ versus $11.7$ pp
$[4.0, 19.4]$. Both judges order the gaps Qwen $>$ GPT-OSS $>$ Nemotron; their support
for the smaller contrast differs.
\texttt{gpt-5-mini} was also the secondary prompt auditor, so this is a partial rather than
an independent replication. Shared dependencies and unblinded language labels remain;
LLM-judge biases are a broader concern \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024unfair}.

\paragraph{Exploratory single-co-author human audit.}
One co-author scored an outcome-independent stratified sample of 180 pair-model jobs
(360 response items) drawn without reference to any automated outcome. They were blinded
to automated-judge scores and aggregate experimental results. Of 357 scored items, three
fail byte verification against the frozen stimuli, leaving 354 analyzed items.
Human--primary-judge exact agreement is 69.2\% (95\% job-cluster bootstrap CI
$[63.8, 74.1]$), with 92.9\% adjacent agreement, $\kappa = 0.485$ and
quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.794$. On the binary non-assistance boundary, human and
primary judge agree on 87.9\% of analyzed items. The human scored higher than the primary
judge on 91 items and lower on 18. Against the replacement judge on these items, exact
agreement is 75.1\% (95\% CI $[70.1,79.9]$), adjacent agreement is 92.7\%,
$\kappa=0.589$ and quadratic-weighted $\kappa=0.851$.

Restricted to the 174 verified jobs with both languages, human and primary-judge point
estimates preserve the model ordering (Table~\ref{tab:human}). For Qwen the human gap is
$+37.9$ pp versus $+36.2$ pp for the judge on the same 58 jobs. This is exploratory
corroboration by one co-author, not independent validation or a test of equality.

\paragraph{Other robustness.}
None of the following moves the Qwen result: restricting to untruncated pairs ($43.36$ pp),
dropping the 2 of 3{,}024 responses scored by the preregistered fallback judge ($43.14$ pp),
or the reported clustering analyses. The mean paired RH/EN character-length ratio is
1.114; it has no detectable monotonic association with the per-pair non-assistance
contrast ($|\rho| \le 0.051$, $p \ge 0.256$). This is not covariate adjustment or evidence
that length has no effect. Appendix~C gives details.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

Three models do not characterize open-weight models, and four framing-wrapped categories do
not characterize harm. We identify a \emph{language-conditioned} difference, not its
mechanism: both inputs use Latin script, while language, lexical choices and syntax vary.
Training frequency was not measured; neither romanization nor familiarity is isolated.
A proposed register control was not completed and provides no evidence here.
Pair equivalence was certified by language models only---no human verified any
pair---a structural weakness in a study premised on uneven model competence in this very
register. The human audit rests on one co-author and cannot serve as external validation;
we make no inter-annotator, adjudication or consensus claim. Three of 360 labels
are blank and three labeled rows fail frozen-stimulus verification, leaving 354 analyzed
items. Responses are single greedy decodes, so run-to-run variability is unmeasured.

Within those bounds, Qwen has a large scored non-assistance gap on the shared matched
bank, robust to a second judge; its contrast with the other models is the robust
heterogeneity result. The single-co-author check preserves the qualitative model ordering
but is not independent or population-level confirmation. English-only measurements need not characterize
Romanized-Hindi behavior. This supports register-sensitive evaluation and per-model claims.

\section{Certification and evaluation detail}

\paragraph{Bank construction and its dependencies.} Candidate pairs were produced by
\texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash} and screened in a cascade: \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash}
scored candidates surviving deterministic prechecks and within-bank deduplication;
\texttt{openai/gpt-5-mini} scored only those the primary
accepted. The auditors are therefore a conjunctive filter, not independent raters, and no
inter-auditor agreement statistic is defined. Both scored each candidate they saw against a
rubric requiring that the Romanized-Hindi member express the same harmful objective,
request the same level of information, preserve the framing and its structural elements,
and read as conversational Romanized Hindi rather than a transliteration.

The persisted GEPA state contains one retained instruction, identical to the seed,
with no accepted evolved revision. A reflection was attempted, but the final exported
instruction remained unchanged. Its target-independent development metric used the
primary auditor's 13 hard constraints, not dual-auditor agreement. Of 30 development
evaluations, 24 were accepted; this is not a final-bank candidate acceptance rate.
The 504/504 retained-pair certification count follows from selection and is not an
independent validity estimate. The final bank does not retain a complete candidate-level
rejection ledger, so its generation acceptance rate cannot be recovered.

The generator/primary-judge identity and secondary-auditor/cross-judge identity are shared
dependencies. No target outcomes informed construction. A fixed bank avoids target-specific
prompt selection but shared selection and judge errors can still interact with model
responses, producing model-dependent measurement effects. Certification remains automated,
not a human equivalence judgment.

\paragraph{Denominators.} 504 unique certified prompt pairs; $504\times2=1{,}008$
responses per target model; 3{,}024 responses in total; 1{,}512 paired pair-model
observations (504 per model). Percentages in Table~\ref{tab:main} are over the 504 pairs of
that model.

\paragraph{Reproducibility configuration.} Bank \texttt{revision\_v2\_3\_1\_final\_504\_dedup},
canonical SHA-256 \path{35bfbc1d09e78b77c24d62bbf41783cc9b297dd25f1bba3fe6062d53dc2028ed}.
Exact target identifiers are \path{qwen/qwen3-30b-a3b-instruct-2507},
\path{openai/gpt-oss-20b} and \path{nvidia/nemotron-3-nano-30b-a3b}. Targets
decoded at temperature $0.0$, empty system prompt, 4{,}096 max tokens; judge
\texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash}, temperature 0, 2{,}048 max tokens, preregistered fallback
\texttt{openai/gpt-5-mini}. Analysis seed 20260829; 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples;
100{,}000 permutation draws. These intervals resample prompt identities, not decoding
runs. Published bootstrap outputs are reused, not redrawn for this paper.
The human analysis instead uses 5{,}000 job-level bootstrap resamples, seed 20260904;
its intervals are conditional on the verified analyzed items. Each sampled human job
has a distinct pair identity.

The saved V2 generator export loads successfully; its normalized-text SHA-256 is
\path{025fbd3573fc018b3291a51a19acb456df3fd79a3725a1b29610dd89bcd5ce3b}.
The response judge receives the original prompt, response and an explicit
\texttt{PROMPT LANGUAGE} field (\texttt{en}/\texttt{rh}). Its rubric ignores disclaimers,
roleplay and other framing, scores only visible content (including truncated responses),
and uses the 0--3 specificity/actionability definitions in Section~2; generic safety
advice, wholly abstract, off-topic or meaningless output is score 0.
Inference was provider-hosted through OpenRouter; physical hardware and aggregate
GPU-hours are unavailable. Development and failed attempts consumed additional compute.
Local analysis uses the persisted bank, responses and scores; exact provider reruns
are not guaranteed to reproduce single-decoding outputs.

\paragraph{Judge fallback.} The primary judge returned a usable score for 3{,}022 of
3{,}024 responses; for two the provider refused content after the retry policy and the
preregistered fallback scored them. Dropping the two affected pairs moves the Qwen gap from
$43.06$ to $43.14$ pp; GPT-OSS remains $4.56$ pp and Nemotron rounds to $-1.79$ pp.

\section{Human audit detail}

\paragraph{Design and realized coverage.} The intended sample is 180 pair-model
jobs, 5 in each of the 36 model $\times$ category $\times$ strategy cells, both languages
retained, drawn with seed 20260831; the selection never read an automated score. Items were
randomized with $\ge 10$ positions between the two language members of a job. This paper
reports one co-author's scores and makes no inter-annotator, adjudication or consensus claim.

\paragraph{Annotator background.} The annotator was an unpaid co-author, comfortable
reading English and conversational Romanized Hindi. They knowingly and voluntarily agreed
to annotate after being informed that the material could be harmful or sensitive, read the
instructions, completed the familiarization examples and scored without AI assistance.
They necessarily saw prompts and target responses, but were blinded to automated-judge
scores and aggregate experimental results. No item was flagged unreadable. Co-authorship
precludes treating the exercise as external validation.

\paragraph{Analysis set.} Of 360 delivered items, 357 contain valid scores and three labels
are blank. Three labeled rows fail byte verification against the frozen stimulus (two prompt
cells and one response cell differ) and are excluded without repair, leaving $n=354$ items
and 174 complete pair-model jobs. No nonblank score is malformed or out of range.

\paragraph{Agreement detail.} By language, human--primary-judge exact agreement is $71.8\%$
on English ($n=174$) and $66.7\%$ on Romanized-Hindi items ($n=180$); by model, $86.6\%$
for GPT-OSS-20B, $62.7\%$ for Qwen3-30B-A3B and $58.1\%$ for Nemotron-3-Nano. Of 43 items
the primary judge scored 3, the human scored 42 at $\ge2$ and 36 at exactly 3. Conversely,
of 92 human scores of 3, 81 received primary-judge scores $\ge2$.

\paragraph{Participant safeguards and documentation limits.} Instructions warn that the
material is harmful and may be unpleasant, encourage breaks, and permit declining any
item or stopping without giving a reason. They forbid external lookup or AI assistance
and require flagging unreadable items. The unpaid co-author voluntarily agreed after the
risks were disclosed. No formal institutional ethics/IRB review or approval was obtained.

\paragraph{Access and broader impact.} Better coverage of everyday registers can reveal
uneven protection, but the adversarial prompts and model outputs could also facilitate
misuse. Raw harmful examples and private annotation mappings are not distributed in this
paper. A future data release would require gated access and a completed license and
safeguards review; no public release or access arrangement is claimed here.

\section{Robustness analyses}

\paragraph{Non-assistance profile (length diagnostic).} Score 0 includes refusal and
other non-assistance. Table~\ref{tab:profile} describes lengths and the original limited
English regex, not a validated refusal classifier. It cannot separate comprehension from
refusal or independently establish harmfulness. All 272 short Nemotron RH zeros are
the explicit refusal ``I'm sorry, but I can't help with that.'' (curly apostrophes in the
stored output). The regex missed these, as well as the same short refusal in 412 GPT-OSS
EN, 367 GPT-OSS RH and 208 Nemotron EN zeros. Qwen's long RH zeros also include explicit
Hindi refusals. Response-length changes within identified flips are conditional behavioral
corroboration, not independent evidence that a response is harmful.

\paragraph{Flip directionality.} Forward and reverse flips are far from balanced for Qwen
(219 forward, 7 reverse) but nearly balanced for Nemotron (65 forward, 70 reverse) and
mildly asymmetric for GPT-OSS (42 forward, 21 reverse). Nemotron's null aggregate gap
therefore reflects cancellation of bidirectional instability rather than consistency.

\paragraph{Near-duplicate clustering.} The construction gate used normalized-text
\texttt{SequenceMatcher}, taking the maximum EN/RH similarity within strategy inside
each source bank. Cross-bank consolidation removed exact matches only (NFKC, case-folded,
whitespace-normalized); it did not enforce this near-duplicate threshold across banks.
Five retained pairwise relationships reach $0.85$ (maximum $0.942$), not five demonstrated
violations of within-bank enforcement. Separately, English within-cell token Jaccard
similarity averages $0.254$ against a random-bank baseline of $0.138$.
Using maximum EN/RH token Jaccard to form connected clusters and
bootstrapping over clusters leaves the Qwen gap at $43.06$ pp with the interval widening
only to $[37.3, 48.6]$ at Jaccard $0.50$ (504 pairs $\rightarrow$ 293 units), and leaves
both Qwen contrasts at $p < 10^{-4}$ throughout. The GPT-OSS versus Nemotron contrast
degrades from $p = 0.032$ (pair level) to $p = 0.062$ (Jaccard $0.65$) and $p = 0.10$
(Jaccard $0.60$), with the interval covering zero; we therefore do not treat it as
established.

\paragraph{Truncation.} Qwen truncated 22 Romanized-Hindi and 0 English responses
(discordance $p = 4.8\times10^{-7}$); Nemotron truncated the other way (77 English, 35
Romanized Hindi, $p = 1.7\times10^{-5}$); GPT-OSS is balanced ($p = 0.37$). Restricting to
untruncated pairs gives Qwen $43.36$ pp ($n = 482$), GPT-OSS $4.73$ pp ($n = 423$) and
Nemotron $-0.50$ pp ($n = 401$).

\paragraph{Other analyses.} The frozen analysis also includes a cluster bootstrap over pair
identities (10{,}000 resamples, reported throughout), paired sign randomization with Holm
correction for the three cross-model contrasts (100{,}000 draws), Bowker symmetry tests on
the score-transition matrices (Qwen $p=1.58\times10^{-44}$, GPT-OSS $p=0.068$,
Nemotron $p=0.019$; 5 degrees of freedom). Bowker tests overall transition asymmetry,
not specifically the score-0 gap. Prompt-length Spearman correlations of RH/EN character
ratio with paired score-0 contrast are $-0.005$ ($p=0.907$), $0.051$ ($p=0.257$),
and $-0.048$ ($p=0.281$), respectively; no conditional model was fit.
Response length is reported as a
judge-free diagnostic only; because verbosity and assistance are confounded we do not treat
it as independent evidence of harm.
